In the classroom — Composing the opera

Composing the opera

Level 2 · Lesson 11 · each group composes the music and the dialogue for one scene of “I’ve Lost My Sandwich!” · the Classical period: Mozart’s dice game

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, in five groups. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each group has its scene ready: two or three lines of dialogue and a piece of music 4 to 8 bars long, lasting less than a minute, in the key of its scene; and the group plays it once for the class.

Secondary objectives: (1) choose major or minor to suit the character of the scene and say why; (2) end the music on the tonic, “at home”.

What you need: the classroom computer, ideally one per group, with the Cosmic Keyboard and the Score; the Melody Machine; the Harmony Machine, which also has waltzes, pasillos and bambucos with dice; the libretto “I’ve Lost My Sandwich!”; one dice per group; and a copy of the group sheet (at the end of this guide) for each pupil. It helps to have done Lessons 8, 9 and 10.

With the two machines, each group can compose several sections for the opera, in the style and with the number of bars it likes best. The score is printed by pressing OPEN IN SCORE and then, in the Score, PRINT.

Before the lesson (15 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Read the libretto aloud and decide which scene you will give to each group.
  • Open the Melody Machine, press SPIN three or four times and listen with PLAY. Try HOLD on a bar you like. Set KEY A and MODE m: the same melody sounds sad. Press SAVE .mmf and open the file in the Score with File › Open.
  • Roll the dice four times with the table in this guide and play the result on the Keyboard (Tonal, middle row).

Before we start: a scene is dialogue and music

The class opera

In an opera the story is told with words and with music. In the class opera there is a narrator who reads the libretto and there are five scenes, one per group. Each scene has two parts: a short dialogue, spoken by the characters, and some music that tells what they feel. The music lasts less than a minute: 8 bars at ♩ = 88 take about 22 seconds, and with the repeat, about 45.

With the Keyboard on Tonal and the KEY and MODE of the scene, the middle row plays the staircase of that key: A is the tonic, home, in every one of them.

The dialogue

Each group writes two or three lines for the characters in its scene, based on the libretto: what Lucy says when she opens her school bag, what her friends ask each other, what the teacher says. The lines are spoken before the music; if the group wants, one line can go over the music, while it plays (in opera this is called melodrama).

Three ways to compose

Each group chooses one. All three end the same way: a melody that begins on chord I, travels through other chords and comes back home, to the tonic.

  1. The Melody Machine. The machine has four reels, one per bar: the first sits on chord I and the last comes back home; in between, each spin chooses its own path of chords (IV, V, VI or II) and writes the name of the chord above each reel. SPIN draws notes and note values at random; HOLD keeps the bar you like; with KEY and MODE it plays in the key of the scene. SAVE .mmf saves the music so it can be opened in the Score. For 8 bars, spin twice and join the two. For a longer scene or act, of 16 bars, spin four times: save each spin with SAVE .mmf and put them one after another in the Score. The group chooses: all 16 bars from the machine, all 16 of their own, or a mixture, with some bars from the machine and some of their own.
  2. The dice. One dice per bar: the number rolled chooses one of the six options in the table. They are written in C major, with the degree below and the Keyboard key above; on Tonal, with the KEY and MODE of the scene, the same keys play in its key. The accompaniment, depending on the scene: bass and chord (the one in the table) for the cheerful or fast scenes; long chords, one per bar, for the sad or calm ones. They are the same accompaniments as in the Harmony Machine (Lesson 10, Part 2).
  3. Our own melody. The group makes it up over the chords, with the rule from Lesson 10: on the first beat of each bar, a note of the chord. Or it transforms the “Ode an die Freude” (Ode to Joy), which the pupils can already play: in minor for sadness (Lesson 8), staccato and fast for the dog, ff with accents for the happy ending (Lesson 9).

The time machine: Mozart’s dice game

1792. A music publisher brings out a game attributed to Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, who had died the year before: Instructions for composing as many waltzes as you like with two dice, without knowing anything about music or composition. For each bar of the waltz there were eleven possible bars, all written over the same harmony, and the dice chose which one. With sixteen bars, there were more waltzes than anyone could play in a whole lifetime. It was a parlour game, and also a lesson: if the harmony is well thought out, chance does not break it. The table in this lesson, and the Melody Machine, work on the same idea.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Next lesson we put on the first performance of an opera, and you are the ones writing the music. Today each group composes its scene: what the characters say and what we hear.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can compose the music for a scene.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: major and minor, I, IV and V

    Play chords I, IV and V in C major on the Keyboard yourself (Z C B · V N A · B M S) and go back to I. “Where do you feel we have arrived home?” On the last one. Change MODE to m and play them again: “Which scene of the opera would sound like this?”

  3. 10 min

    New material: the story and the three ways

    1. The story (4 min). Read the libretto aloud yourself, doing the voices. Hand out the scenes and write the table of keys on the board.
    2. The three ways (6 min). Show each one yourself: one spin of the Melody Machine with PLAY; one roll of the dice with the table, played on the Keyboard; and the beginning of the Ode in minor, with KEY A and MODE m. Each group chooses its way.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the machine and the table on the screen; without a screen, each group works with the printed sheet.

  4. 27 min

    Application: each group composes its scene

    Composing (15 min). Each group composes 4 or 8 bars in its chosen way and writes them on the group sheet, with key letters or degrees. Go round the groups and ask: “Does your music sound the way the character feels?” If not, they change a bar, the tempo or the mode.

    The dialogue (5 min). They write two or three lines for their characters and decide who says them.

    Into the Score (7 min). The Melody Machine groups save with SAVE .mmf and open the file in the Score; the others write their melody in the top channel and the chord for each bar in the one below, as in Lesson 10. They choose an instrument and one or two dynamics (Lesson 9).

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal: the first rehearsal

    In the order of the story, each group says its dialogue and plays its music, live or from the computer, once. The class hears the whole opera for the first time.

    “In which scene was the music most like what was happening? Did they all get home?”

Challenge for the quick ones: “Play your melody with the KEY and MODE of another scene. Does what it tells change?” And one more: “Three well-known tunes are hidden in the dice table. Which are they, and in which boxes?” The Ode (bar 1, option 3), “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” (bars 1 and 2, option 4) and its ending (bar 4, option 5).

In the workbook: chapter 12 and the first night

The pupil’s workbook covers this lesson in six pages: the opera and its scenes; the three ways to compose (the Melody Machine, the dice, and their own melody or the transformed Ode); the dice table from this guide; and the group sheet, with the dialogue and the melodrama.

The last two pages prepare the first night as a show for everyone. Each pupil chooses a job: narrators and librettists (literature), set designers, costumes, dancers, choir and Suite technicians (the Score on FULL, the Keyboard live, the Melody Machine). And there is a printed programme that brings together the work of the year: an overture with the Seikilos with chords; the five scenes; a dance interlude with Rocinante’s gallop by Telemann; a percussion interlude, the drum circle from pp to fff; and the grand finale with the “Ode to Joy” with dynamics, sung by everyone. The programme is only a suggestion: each school puts it together to suit itself.

If they find it hard:
  • They can’t agree: each pupil chooses one bar (rolls the dice or does a spin with HOLD) and the group puts them together.
  • The music doesn’t end: remind them of the rule: the last note is the tonic, the A or K key on Tonal.
  • They go over a minute: 8 bars and one repeat at most.
  • There isn’t time for the Score: the rehearsal can be played on the Keyboard; the Score is finished at the start of the next lesson.

Going further: the Dice Machine

From Mozart’s game to the pasillo and the bambuco

The Dice Machine is the second page of the Harmony Machine (you move from one to the other with the ‹ › arrows in the title). The reels suggest the melody, as in the Melody Machine; the dice do not choose the notes but how they are accompanied: as a waltz, a pasillo or a bambuco. Choose WALTZ, PASILLO or BAMBUCO, press SPIN and PLAY. Above each card appear the two dice for that bar.

What the dice stand for

Each bar rolls two dice. The total goes from 2 to 12, but not every total comes up equally often: with two dice there are 36 combinations, and 7 can be made in six ways (1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1), while 2 and 12 can be made in only one. That is why the machine keeps the unusual for the rare boxes and the ordinary for the common ones. This is the machine’s table, the same one that appears below the cards: each bar rolls its two dice, finds its box and plays the pattern written there.

The bass to the 5th is only used when that fifth belongs to the chord; if not, the bar stays as usual. In the pasillo and the bambuco, “the usual” is their own pattern (see below), and the hemiola is the same idea: two dotted-crotchet basses, on 1 and on the “and” of 2, with the chord between them.

The hemiola (↔) is the bar of three split into two equal halves: instead of ONE-two-three you hear ONE-and-TWO-and. It is a surprise that composers use sparingly, just before an ending or to give the music a push. In the machine it comes up in roughly one bar in six.

Dice and maths: the formula in a picture

At the top, the machine’s rule in one line. In the middle, how many ways there are to roll each total: the towers form a mountain with its peak at 7. At the bottom, the same idea in music: D at the centre of the natural notes.

The table with letters

Let us call the number on the white dice a and the number on the red dice b. Each box is a pair (a, b): (2, 5) means “white 2, red 5”. The total is s = a + b.

An example. Amy rolls white 2 and red 5: s = 2 + 5 = 7. Her distance from 7 is d = |7 − 7| = 0, and her bar is “the usual”. Tom rolls white 6 and red 5: s = 11 and d = |11 − 7| = 4. Hemiola! The bars | | mean “how far, whichever side”: 9 is 2 away from 7, and so is 5.

The machine’s rule fits in one line: d = |a + b − 7|. If d is 0 or 1, the usual; if it is 2, bass to the 5th; if it is 3, held bass; if it is 4 or 5, hemiola.

How many ways there are to roll each total: m = 6 − |s − 7|. Try it: for s = 7, 6 − 0 = 6; for s = 10, 6 − 3 = 3. Count the boxes in the table and you will see that it is true.

The fractions. Counting the colours: 16 + 8 + 6 + 6 = 36. The usual comes up 16 times out of 36 (16/36 = 4/9); bass to the 5th, 8/36 = 2/9; held bass, 6/36 = 1/6; hemiola, 6/36 = 1/6. All four together add up to 36/36 = 1: one of them comes up on every roll.

The geometry of the table

Now let us look at the table as a picture.

  • The diagonals. The boxes with the same total form a slanting line. The line for 7 is the longest: six boxes, from one corner to the other. 2 and 12 have just one box each.
  • The mirror. If we fold the table along the diagonal from (1, 1) to (6, 6), each box lands on another of the same colour: (2, 5) lands on (5, 2). Changing the order of the dice does not change the total.
  • The half turn. If we turn the table half a turn around its centre, it looks the same: (1, 2) ends up at (6, 5). The total 3 becomes 11, and both are 4 away from 7.
  • The mountain. With the totals in a row and a tower for each way of rolling them, you get the mountain in the picture: it climbs up to 7 and comes down the same way on the other side.

7 and D: two centres

On the line of fifths of the natural notes, F C G D A E B, D is in the middle: three notes on each side. G and A are a fifth away from D, one on each side, just as 6 and 8 are one away from 7. C and E are two away; F and B, three, at the ends. In both cases what counts is the distance from the centre, not the side.

“Which pair of notes is furthest from D? And which pair of totals is furthest from 7?”

Three ways to accompany in 3/4

  • The waltz: the bass on 1 and the chord on 2 and 3: OOM-pah-pah. It is the ballroom dance of 19th-century Europe.
  • The pasillo: the bass on 1 and on 3; the chord on the “and” of 1, on the “and” of 2 and on 3. Beat 2 stays silent, and that is why the pasillo sounds hurried, as if it were running ahead. It was born in Colombia and Ecuador, from the waltz.
  • The bambuco: the bass on 1 and on 3; the chord on the “and” of 2 and on the “and” of 3. The chords fall between the beats: this is the syncopation that makes a bambuco, so typical that people still argue about whether it should be written in 3/4 or in 6/8.
  • With JAZZ: the waltz changes its clothes: the double bass walks in crotchets, note by note, and the piano comes in on 2.

The map of the form

A pasillo, a bambuco or a waltz is not just 16 bars: it is two parts in conversation. Part A presents the theme in the main key; part B brings another theme in a neighbouring key; at the end we go back to A and finish there. Each part has two 8-bar phrases, the question and the answer, and each part is repeated.

What the Dice Machine does today is part A: 16 bars, question and answer. With two spins, one for A and another for B (in the neighbouring key), and A again at the end, the class already has a complete pasillo.

The game in the classroom, with real dice

Each group needs a white dice and a red one (or two of different colours) and the table. For each bar of their melody, they roll both dice, find the box and write the pattern they got under the bar. Then they press SPIN on the machine and look at the table below the cards: each number is a bar in its box. The machine does the same as they do, only faster. At the end, they count: which pattern came up most often in the whole class? Why?

Let’s count: “Press SPIN three times and count how many hemiolas ↔ came up in the 16 bars. How many did you expect?” (one in six: about two or three per spin). And an announcement: in Lesson 14 we are going to count how many different waltzes could come out of Mozart’s game. It is a 17-digit number.

The workbook appendix: the maths of music

Who it is for and when to use it

At the end of the pupil’s workbook (pages 56 to 61) there is a six-page appendix: a prelude with Pythagoras and five worksheets. It goes over, in children’s words, what this guide explains in “Dice and maths”. You do not need to be a maths teacher to teach it: everything is solved by counting boxes, adding two dice and using a calculator. If there is a maths teacher at the school, it is a good opportunity to work on it together, but it is not necessary.

When: after this lesson, once the class has played with the Dice Machine. It can be taught in three sessions of about 40 minutes (prelude and worksheet 1 · worksheets 2 and 3 · worksheets 4 and 5) or shared out as homework with the family. It is extension material: if there is no time, the programme carries on without it.

What you need: one light and one dark dice per group (for example, a white one and a red one), one calculator per group (a phone calculator will do), and the computer with the Dice Machine, the Melody Machine and the Keyboard.

Page by page

Prelude · Pythagoras and the music of the spheres. Read the story together. What matters is a single idea: sounds that go well together are simple numbers (half the string gives the octave; two thirds, the fifth). If you have a string instrument in the classroom (guitar, violin), press a string down exactly in the middle and let them hear it.

“Can you hear a sound that is always there, so that you don’t notice it any more?” (the fridge, the traffic, the clock…)

Worksheet 1 · Why is 7 the king of the dice? Have each group find all the boxes in the table that add up to 7 and count them (there are 6), and then the ones that add up to 2 and 12 (one each). The mountain of bars is just that count, drawn. Step 3 introduces the distance from 7: it does not matter whether the total is above or below, only how many steps away from 7 it is.

“If we roll 36 times, how many times do you expect to get 7? And 12?” (about 6 times for 7; once for 12)

Worksheet 2 · How the machine plays. Show one spin on the Dice Machine (WALTZ, SPIN and PLAY): above each card appear the two dice for that bar. Then each group does the “Let’s play!”: they roll their dice 4 times and colour in each bar. At the end, have the whole class count how many greens came up: they should be the majority.

Worksheet 3 · The dice choose the melody. Now one dice and a table with 4 columns: each roll chooses one bar of the melody. Have each group roll 4 times, copy out their melody and play it on the Keyboard (the key letters are above each note). The 1296 melodies come from multiplying: 6 options in each of the 4 bars, 6 × 6 × 6 × 6.

Worksheet 4 · D, the centre of the notes. Write F C G D A E B on the board and, underneath, 4 5 6 7 8 9 10: the two centres line up one above the other. On the Keyboard, show that D is between the two black keys and play from D to D: the staircase reads the same going up and coming down.

“Which pair of notes is furthest from D? And which pair of totals is furthest from 7?” (F and B; 4 and 10)

Worksheet 5 · The numbers of sound. With the calculator. First the octave (× 2) and the fifth (× 1.5); then the comma: have them key in 1.5 × 1.5 × … twelve times (or 1.5 to the power of 12) and 2 × 2 × … seven times, and compare. In the Melody Machine there is the tower of fifths game: with the 13th fifth the tower falls down, and that is the same “little bit” left over on the calculator.

The answers

  • Worksheet 1: 36 pairs (6 × 6). 7 comes up in 6 ways: 1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1. 2 and 12, in only one. A total of 5 and a total of 9 are both 2 steps away from 7.
  • Worksheet 2: 16 greens + 8 golds + 6 oranges + 6 wine-reds = 36. The surprise (wine-red) comes up about 1 bar in every 6. In the “Let’s play!” there is no single answer: each group colours in what it rolled.
  • Worksheet 3: 6 × 6 × 6 × 6 = 1296 melodies. The three hidden tunes: the Ode (bar 1, row 3), “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” (bars 1 and 2, row 4) and the ending of “Twinkle, Twinkle” (bar 4, row 5).
  • Worksheet 4: G and A are 1 step from D; C and E, 2; F and B, 3. The steps from D to D: 1 · ½ · 1 · 1 · 1 · ½ · 1.
  • Worksheet 5: B = 330 × 1.5 = 495. G = 195.6 × 2 = 391.1. Twelve fifths: 1.5¹² ≈ 129.75; seven octaves: 2⁷ = 128; 129.75 ÷ 128 ≈ 1.0136: that is the Pythagorean comma.
If they find it hard:
  • The bars | | in d = | a + b − 7 |: they just mean “the distance, whichever side”. Put it like this: “how many steps is it from 7 to your total?” 5 and 9 are both 2 steps away. The bars only appear in the “For the curious” box: anyone who doesn’t understand it can skip it and still do all the worksheets.
  • “Why 7?”: because it has the most ways to get there. Have them check it by rolling: across the whole class, 7 comes up more than any other total.
  • The fractions (16/36, 1/6): there is no need to work them out. It is enough to say “16 rolls in every 36” or “one in six”.
  • × 1.5: it is “one and a half times”: the number plus its half. 440 + 220 = 660. And “dividing by 2 to come back home” is going down an octave, like singing the same note lower.
  • The calculator shows 129.746…: that is fine; it is rounded to 129.75. What matters is that it does not give 128: there is a little bit left over, and that little bit can be heard.

Bars and time signatures

Level 1 · Lesson 7 · the march, the waltz, the song and the two numbers

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 8 to 12, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair listens to a melody, finds out with their body whether it is a march, a waltz or a song, and writes its time signature with the two numbers (2/4, 3/4 or 4/4) on the board; the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) show the strong beat with the body in groups of 2, 3 and 4; (2) say what the two numbers of the time signature mean: the top one, how many beats; the bottom one, that the beat is a crotchet.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet access, the board, space to move and, if available, a projector. It helps to have done Lessons 5 and 6 first. No music knowledge needed: the keyboard does the work; you guide.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise the keyboard. In class your attention needs to be on the children, not on the buttons. Repeat these steps until you can do them without looking at the guide:

  • Open the Cosmic Keyboard and the metronome (pulse, about 60 BPM with tap, start and click, as in Lesson 1). Count out loud “one, two”, stressing the one; then “one, two, three” and “one, two, three, four”.
  • Play the march and the waltz in this guide, using the keys written above each note.
  • Open “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” in the Score: at the beginning it has a C, which is another way of writing 4/4. Remember ⛶ FULL and − for projecting, and the ♩ PULSE knob for the tempo (Lesson 4). Leave both tabs open.
  • Look at the pupil’s worksheet at the end: you can project it or print it.

Before we start: the two numbers

Strong and weak: the bar

When we walk, dance or sing, not all beats weigh the same: the first of each group is stronger. That group of beats is the bar, and in the score it is marked off by a vertical bar line. At the end of the piece there is a double bar line.

The big circle is the strong beat, the “one” of each bar.

Top: how many; bottom: which one

The time signature is written with two numbers, one above the other. The top number says how many beats there are in each bar: 2, 3 or 4. The bottom number says which note value is the beat: when there is a 4 at the bottom, the beat is the crotchet, a quarter of the chocolate bar (Lesson 5). That is why 4/4 is called “four-four” time: four beats, each one a quarter.

4/4 is so common that it has its own shorthand: a large C.

4/4 and C say the same thing: four crotchet beats in each bar.

Did you know…? If there were a 2 at the bottom, the beat would be the minim, half the chocolate bar. They will see this in Level 2.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Today we are going to discover that beats come in groups, and that those groups are written with two numbers. At the end, every pair will listen to a melody and write its two numbers on the board.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can find and write the time signature of a melody.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: ta, ti-ti and rest

    You read and clap one of the rhythms from Lesson 6; the class repeats it. Then the class reads and claps the other one.

    “Do all the beats feel the same, or is one of them stronger?”

  3. 17 min

    New material: the march, the waltz, the song and the two numbers

    1. The march: in 2s (4 min). Show it yourself first: march on the spot saying “one, two”, with your left foot on the one. The class follows you while you play the example march.

    Example march in 2 time (a Music Motion melody).

    1. The waltz: in 3s (4 min). Sway from side to side: “one, two, three”; on the one you lean over, on two and three you come back. The class follows you while you play the waltz. The pasillo and the bambuco from Colombia are also counted in 3s: if the class knows one, use it.

    Example waltz in 3 time (a Music Motion melody). The last note is a dotted minim: it lasts three beats.

    1. The song: in 4s (3 min). Clap on the one and tap your knees on two, three and four. Switch to the score tab, press ▶ on “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” and the class keeps time with that gesture.
    2. The two numbers (6 min). Write 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 on the board, side by side. The class says what the top number means (how many beats: the march, the waltz, the song). Introduce the bottom number with the chocolate bar: “the 4 tells us that the beat is the crotchet, a quarter of the chocolate bar”. Show the C at the beginning of “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” on the screen: “it is another way of writing 4/4”.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the drawings and the score on the screen; without a screen, copy them onto the board.

  4. 20 min

    Application: march, waltz or song?

    Guess the time signature (12 min). Show it yourself first: play the waltz, move with it and write 3/4 on the board, saying “it sways in threes; the beat is the crotchet”.

    1. Play one of the three melodies without saying which. The whole class moves: do they march, sway, or clap and tap their knees?
    2. A pair comes up to the board and writes the two numbers.
    3. The class gives a thumbs-up if the time signature is correct.
    4. Repeat with other melodies and other pairs. For “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star”, use the score; you can turn the ♩ PULSE knob to play it slower or faster: the time signature does not change.

    Reading the time signature in the score (8 min). With “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” on the screen, the class looks for the clef, the C time signature, the bar lines and the final double bar line. Ask: “How many bars does the song have?” Answer: 12. Then they count the beats in one bar, pointing to each note: the minims are worth two.

    The bar dance (if there is time left). Play the march and then the waltz. The class dances: in the march, a strong step on every “one”; in the waltz, a turn or a sway on the “one” and two soft little steps. Everyone counts the bars out loud: at bar 8, everyone finishes together with a pose. This is how the class discovers that the bar is what puts the dance in order. The same game is in the pupil’s workbook, My Little Music Grammar · Level 1 (chapter 7, exercise 5).

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Play one last melody; the pairs who have not had a turn yet write its time signature, and the class gives a thumbs-up if it matches.

    “How did you know whether it was a march, a waltz or a song? What does the top number say? And the bottom one?”

    Listen to the answers: finding the strong beat with the body, counting until the one comes back, and that the 4 at the bottom is the crotchet.

Challenge for fast finishers: “Twinkle, Twinkle, box by box”. The pairs who finish early copy the table below onto their sheet (one beat per box) and play the first four bars: one plays and the other points to the box. The dash means that the previous note keeps sounding: it is a minim.

With the Melody Machine: the machine’s time signature

To close the lesson, or to start the next one. Open the Melody Machine and press SPIN.

  1. March, waltz or song? Press PLAY: the class marks the beat with their bodies and works out how many beats each bar has. The machine’s melodies are in 4/4: a song.
  2. The first beat. PLAY again: a strong clap on the first beat of each bar and a soft one on the other three.
  3. The bar lines. On the screen, each reel is a bar and the vertical line at the end is the bar line. How many bars does the melody have?
If they find it hard:
  • They cannot find the strong beat: count out loud yourself and exaggerate the one with your voice and your foot; then stop counting and let them keep feeling it with their bodies.
  • They mix up march and song (2 and 4): have them count out loud until the strong one comes back: “one, two, one” or “one, two, three, four, one”. The song has more steps.
  • They swap the numbers round: “top: how many; bottom: which one”. Write it in a corner of the board.
  • They get lost counting the bars in the score: have them put a finger on each bar line and count the bar lines, not the notes.

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs write the correct time signature for the melody they heard, and the class confirms it with a thumbs-up. In addition, most pupils show the strong beat with their body and say what each number means.

These lessons are a starting point. Each teacher can design the examples to suit their own class. For our part, we will keep adding lesson topics according to the requests we receive through the contact form.

Bars and time signatures, at home

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we learned that beats come in groups: of 2 (march), of 3 (waltz) or of 4 (song), and that each group is written with two numbers. This week, play together:

  1. March, waltz or song? Put on some music you like (the radio, a family favourite). The child finds the strong beat and decides whether to march, sway, or clap and tap their knees. Many songs are in 4; waltzes, and the Colombian pasillo and bambuco, are in 3.
  2. The number dance: one person says “2”, “3” or “4”; the other walks, counting, and stamps hard on every one. Then swap.
  3. Twinkle, Twinkle: sing “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” together with a clap on the one and taps on the knees on two, three and four.
  4. The Melody Machine: open musicmotionsuite.com/suite/tragamonedas.html, press SPIN and PLAY and mark the beat: strong on the first beat of each bar. How many beats are there in each bar? With PRINT you can print the melody. No registration needed.

Music in the classroom

Programme guide · Level 1 and Level 2

For the teacher and the school management. © 2026 Music Motion.

What it is: two levels of weekly lessons that add to the music programme the school already has. Each level has about ten 50-minute lessons.

Who it is for: Level 1, ages 8 to 12; Level 2, ages 9 to 14. They are designed for teachers who are not musicians: the keyboard, the tuner and the Score do the work; the teacher guides.

What it brings together: music as a meeting point of the curriculum. Its lessons cross language (the verses and the opera libretto), drama and the visual arts (the class opera, with its staging and its masks), mathematics (the fractions of the bar, the dice table and the count of possible melodies), geometry (the symmetries of the table, D at the centre of the natural notes) and history (in every lesson, a time machine: from the Seikilos column to Mozart).

What is needed: the minimum is a laptop with an extra speaker, so the whole class can hear well, and the board. If the classroom has a digital whiteboard, an interactive screen or a projector, so much the better; if not, the lessons work just the same. In Level 2 one computer per group of 4 or 5 pupils is advisable. The Cosmic Keyboard is free; the Score and the rest of the suite are used with the school account.

If a button is not clear: the user guide explains each one with pictures. At the top it has a search box: type, for example, “pulse”, “print” or “wav”.

Why: musical literacy

A paradox

No society disputes that every child should learn to read and write: literacy is a right and the basis of everything else. With music there is a paradox. We all hear it from the cradle, we sing it, dance to it and use it to celebrate, to remember and to say goodbye; yet reading and writing it is left to a specialised minority. We treat music as a language that almost everyone speaks and almost no one learns to read or write.

“Music is a human birthright,” says the opening of Elements of Music, the guide by Mario Maurano on which this programme is based. If it is a right, so is understanding it.

A little music grammar

The programme proposes a small grammar of music —a “little music grammar”— to make sounds literate in the classroom, just as words are. Its aims:

  • Reading. Recognising on the staff the pitch of the notes, their duration, the time signature and the tempo, as one recognises letters and words.
  • Writing. Notating a rhythm and a melody, and composing a bar of one’s own. In Level 1 each pupil writes one; in Level 2, the whole class composes a small opera.
  • Listening with understanding. Hearing any genre —the family’s music, the radio’s, an orchestra’s— while understanding how it is made. Music is our history, and being able to read it means being able to enter it.
  • Thinking. Music is a place where numbers can be heard: the note values are fractions of a chocolate bar, the bar is a sum and the tempo is a number of beats per minute.
  • For everyone. No prior talent or instrument of one’s own: the computer keyboard serves as the instrument and the teacher does not need to be a musician.

Literacy does not mean training professional musicians, just as teaching to read does not mean training writers: it means giving everyone the key to a common language. The guides say so to the parents on every take-home sheet, with ten-minute games that require no musical knowledge.

Music among the arts

Music never comes to the classroom alone: it has always lived alongside the other arts. The programme does not replace the poetry, drama or visual arts the school already teaches: it brings them together around music.

  • With poetry. Level 1 ends with verses: the class melody is repeated and each child gives it a stanza. Verse and bar adapt to each other, and the children discover that syllables have stress, like the beats of the bar.
  • With drama and the visual arts. Level 2 ends with a small opera, which brings all the arts together in a single work: the music, the poetry of the libretto, the staging, and the set, costumes and masks that the pupils themselves can paint.
  • With history. Every Level 2 lesson opens a “time machine”: a period and a work that travel to the classroom, from the Seikilos column in Antiquity to Mozart’s dice game. Music is learned together with the moment it was born.
  • With mathematics. In lesson 11 the machine’s dice become a table of 36 squares: the sum, the distance to 7 written as a formula, fractions, and the geometry of the table (its diagonals, its mirror and its half turn), with 7 at the centre as D is at the centre of the natural notes. In lesson 14 the count grows: how many melodies fit, and how many waltzes came out of Mozart’s game.

That is why the music teacher does not have to do it alone. The language, art, drama and mathematics teachers can join in with what they already teach, and the opera becomes a project for the whole year group.

What each lesson is like

  1. It starts with a review

    The first 5 to 10 minutes go back over the previous lesson. Topics return in a spiral: what is introduced one week is used in the following ones.

  2. It moves step by step

    The teacher’s guide divides the lesson into five or six moments, each with its minutes, what is done, what is played and the questions for the class.

  3. It ends at the keyboard

    Every lesson closes with a moment at the computer keyboard. In Level 2 the Score is added: the pupils see on the staff what they play and hear it note by note.

  4. It continues at home

    A cut-out sheet invites parents to play for 10 minutes a week with their children, without knowing music.

How to know it worked: each guide ends with a simple sign the teacher can observe in class, without a test.

Level 1 · listening, reading and counting

Ages 8 to 12 · 9 lessons

From the beat and pitch to the staff, the note values and the bar. Arithmetic appears without turning into a maths lesson: the semibreve is a chocolate bar that breaks into minims, crotchets and quavers, and the number of the time signature is counted with the body before it is written.

How it fits the school’s programme

  • One lesson a week, within the music period or as a workshop. A level takes a long term or a semester.
  • The order can be adapted: the teacher can repeat a lesson, split it in two or skip one the class already knows.
  • The examples are a starting point: each teacher can replace them with songs from their region or their class.
  • Either level can come first: Level 2 opens with a review of all of Level 1.

Technical requirements

For the management and the IT coordinator

  • The minimum: a laptop with a keyboard and an extra speaker. Everything opens in the browser (Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox), on Windows, Mac or Chromebook, with nothing to install. The sound is generated by the computer itself.
  • With a digital whiteboard or interactive screen: the laptop is connected to the screen, and the whole class sees the keyboard, the metronome and, in Level 2, the staff of the Score as it plays. The sound drawings can be made directly on the screen.
  • Without a screen or projector: the laptop with its speaker is enough. The teacher plays, the class listens and answers with the body, and the drawings are made on the ordinary board. No lesson depends on the pupils seeing the screen.
  • The guide on the phone: the teacher can follow each lesson’s guide on their phone while the computer plays; the pages adapt to the small screen. It can also be printed as a PDF.
  • The physical keyboard: the lessons use the computer keys (Z, X, C…). A tablet on its own, without a keyboard, does not work the same way.
  • The internet filter: if the page does not load, musicmotionsuite.com and, for the tuner, tuner.mariomaurano.com must be allowed. It is a one-time request.
  • The microphone: it is used by the tuner; the browser asks for permission the first time. On school-managed Chromebooks, the IT coordinator may need to enable it.
  • Without a good connection: it is enough to share the teacher’s phone connection with the laptop (hotspot). Once open, the suite keeps working even if the signal drops. If the school has no connection at all, Music Motion can arrange a version for offline use on the teacher’s laptop, tied to the school licence.

What the school receives

Once the order is confirmed and the school account is activated, Music Motion sends the programme for the chosen levels:

  • the teacher’s guides for each lesson, to open in the browser and as a PDF to print;
  • the take-home sheets, one per lesson;
  • the example melodies for each level, ready to open in the Score;
  • the libretto of the opera “I’ve Lost My Sandwich!”, with the narrator and the scenes.

The options: the school chooses

  1. The levels: Level 1, Level 2 or both.
  2. The language of the material: Spanish, English, French, Dutch or German; one or several.
  3. The format: digital, or digital plus a printed set.
  4. An introductory session for the teachers (optional), online or at the school.
  5. Example melodies of your own (optional), for instance songs from the region, prepared to order. Price on request.

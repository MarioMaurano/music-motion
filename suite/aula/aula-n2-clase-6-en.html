In the classroom — Intervals

Intervals

Level 2 · Lesson 6 · from the second to the octave, major, minor and perfect, and inversion · Impressionism with Ravel

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair names six intervals written on the stave (the number and whether it is major, minor or perfect) and inverts them with the rule of 9; the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) find a second and a third in Ravel’s melody, by listening and looking at the score; (2) sing a second, a fifth and an octave with the help of a song.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, the Cosmic Keyboard, the 2nd movement of Ravel’s Concerto in G in the Score, the board and, if possible, a projector. It helps to have done Lessons 4 and 5.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • On the Keyboard, play C (Z) with each note of the scale, one by one: X, C, V, B, N, M and the high C (A). These are the 2nd, the 3rd, the 4th, the 5th, the 6th, the 7th and the 8th.
  • Open Ravel’s Concerto and listen to the first minute with your eyes on the melody, the top stave. After 5 seconds (bar 2) it goes up from G♯ to A; at 24 seconds (bar 6), B, C♯, B and down to G♯; at 48 seconds (bar 11) it goes up C♯, E, F♯, A, C♯. With the ♩ PULSE knob you can make it slower.
  • Try out the rule of 9 with two or three intervals from the table.

Before we start: the distance between two notes

Counting steps

An interval is the distance between two notes. To name it, you count all the notes, including the bottom one and the top one: from C to E there are C, D, E, three notes: a third. From C to G, five: a fifth. From C to the next C, eight: an octave. On the stave you can see it without counting: a third goes from a line to the next line, or from a space to the next space; a second, from a line to a space.

From C: the 2nd, the 3rd, the 4th, the 5th, the 6th, the 7th and the 8th.

Major, minor and perfect

Two thirds can have the same number and a different size. C–E has two tones: it is a major third. D–F has a tone and a diatonic semitone: it is a minor third. The 2nd, the 3rd, the 6th and the 7th are major or minor; the 4th, the 5th and the 8th are perfect: they sound pure, because they come from the simplest fractions, 4/3, 3/2 and 2/1. A third made smaller by one more chromatic semitone is diminished: Bach’s in Lesson 5. Made bigger, it is augmented.

Counting in the scale

The pupil’s workbook draws the scale of C with the name of each step: tone, tone, diatonic semitone, tone, tone, tone, diatonic semitone. A major 2nd is a tone, made of a chromatic and a diatonic semitone; a minor 2nd is just a diatonic semitone. A major 3rd has two tones; a minor 3rd, a tone and a diatonic semitone.

The exercise asks them to count them in the scale of C: 5 major seconds (C–D, D–E, F–G, G–A, A–B) and 2 minor (E–F, B–C); 3 major thirds (C–E, F–A, G–B) and 4 minor (D–F, E–G, A–C, B–D). Then, with the rule of 9: the major 2nd becomes a minor 7th, the minor 2nd a major 7th, the major 3rd a minor 6th and the minor 3rd a major 6th.

Inversion: the rule of 9

To invert an interval is to move the bottom note up an octave: C–E becomes E–C. The new number comes from taking the old one away from 9: an inverted third is a sixth (9 − 3 = 6); a second, a seventh (9 − 2 = 7); a fourth, a fifth. And the kind changes: major becomes minor, minor becomes major, perfect stays perfect, and augmented and diminished swap over.

C–E, a major 3rd; inverted, E–C, a minor 6th. 9 − 3 = 6, and major becomes minor.

Did you know…? Why 9 and not 8? Because when you count both notes, the one in the middle is counted twice: a third plus a sixth fill an octave, and 3 + 6 = 9. The octave has eight notes, but the meeting note is in both.

The time machine: Impressionism with Ravel

Paris, 1931. Maurice Ravel finishes his Concerto in G for piano and orchestra; the pianist Marguerite Long gives the first performance in 1932. Ravel and Claude Debussy are the great names of musical Impressionism: like the Impressionist painters with light, they look for the colour and the atmosphere of sound. The second movement begins with the piano alone: a long, calm melody over an accompaniment that sways in three beats. Ravel said that this melody, which seems so easy, cost him a great effort to write, bar by bar.

The melody almost always moves in small intervals, seconds and thirds, and that is why it sounds like someone singing softly.

Bar 2 (5 seconds): G♯ → A, a minor 2nd, a diatonic semitone.

Bars 6 and 7 (24 seconds): B → C♯ → B, a major 2nd that goes up and comes back; B → G♯, a minor 3rd going down.

Bar 11 (48 seconds): C♯–E minor 3rd, E–F♯ major 2nd, F♯–A minor 3rd, A–C♯ major 3rd. Together, an octave.

In those countries, instead of Ravel, listen to Claude Debussy’s “Clair de lune” (1905), also Impressionist and in the public domain: the demo is coming soon.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Today we are going to measure the distance between two notes, give it a name and turn it upside down. At the end, each pair is going to name six intervals and invert them.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can name an interval and invert it.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: tone and semitone

    Play C–D (Z X) and E–F (C V) yourself. “Which one is a tone and which one is the small step? What is it called on the piano? And in the voice, what kind of semitone is E–F?” A semitone on the piano; in the voice, a diatonic semitone, because the note changes its name. Write the scale of C on the board and mark the diatonic semitones E–F and B–C.

  3. 17 min

    New material: counting, measuring and inverting

    1. Counting steps (5 min). Write C and E on the stave and count out loud yourself: “C, D, E: three, a third”. The class counts C–G and C–C with you. Then one half of the class says a pair and the other half counts. Golden rule: count both end notes.
    2. Major, minor, perfect (6 min). On the scale on the board, count the tones in C–E (two: major) and in D–F (a tone and a diatonic semitone: minor). Play them one after the other: the major third sounds more open; the minor one, darker. The class says “major” or “minor” for three more pairs.
    3. The rule of 9 (6 min). With two pupils at the front, one is C and the other E: C climbs over E and ends up on top. “What interval is it now?” Count: E, F, G, A, B, C: a sixth. “9 − 3 = 6.” Write the rule on the board and repeat it with a second and a fourth.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the staves and Ravel’s score on the screen; without a screen, copy them onto the board.

  4. 20 min

    Application: Ravel, the songs and the sheet

    Ravel (8 min). Tell the story of the Concerto. Project the score with ⛶ FULL and press ▶; the class listens to the first minute while watching the melody. Go back to the beginning and stop at bar 2: “G♯, A: what interval?” A 2nd. At bar 6, “B, G♯”: a 3rd. A question about the period: “What colours does this music make you imagine?”

    The songs (4 min). Play the 2nd, the 5th and the 8th on the Keyboard yourself and sing the beginning of their song; the class sings with you.

    The sheet (8 min). Each pair names the six intervals on the sheet and inverts them with the rule of 9. Do the first one yourself on the board.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Read out the six intervals on the sheet yourself; the pairs say the name and the inversion, and the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

    “How do you count an interval? What is the difference between a major third and a minor third? What does a third become when you invert it?”

    Listen to the answers: with both end notes; a diatonic semitone; a sixth.

Challenge for the quick ones: “Invert Bach’s diminished third, A♭–F♯ (F♯ below, A♭ above).” 9 − 3 = 6, and diminished becomes augmented: an augmented sixth, A♭–F♯. And one with numbers: on the tonal staircase, a major 3rd (81/64) times a minor 6th (128/81) gives 2: the octave.
If they find it hard:
  • They count one note too few: have them put a finger on every line and every space, from the first note to the last, both included.
  • They confuse major and minor: have them count the diatonic semitones in the scale on the board (E–F, B–C): for thirds, if the interval contains one, it is minor.
  • The rule of 9 seems like magic to them: draw the octave from C to C and mark the third and the sixth: together they fill it, and the meeting note is counted twice.
  • They can’t follow Ravel’s melody: slow it down with the ♩ PULSE knob and point to the notes on the screen yourself.

The distance between two notes

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we learned to measure the distance between two notes, the interval: you count the notes from one to the other, both included. A third can be major or minor, and when you turn it upside down it becomes a sixth: the rule of 9. This week, play together:

  1. The interval song: one person sings the beginning of “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” and the other says what interval it is (a fifth). Look together for other songs that begin with a big leap.
  2. The rule of 9: one person says an interval (“a second”) and the other answers with its inversion in less than three seconds (“a seventh”).
  3. Ravel: listen together to the 2nd movement of Ravel’s Concerto in G (search for “Ravel Piano Concerto in G Adagio”). What colours does it make you imagine?

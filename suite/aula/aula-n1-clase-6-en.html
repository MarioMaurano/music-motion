In the classroom — Quavers and rests

Quavers and rests

Level 1 · Lesson 6 · “ti-ti” and the rest you count

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 8 to 12, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair reads aloud, with rhythm syllables, a one-bar rhythm written on the board (crotchets, quavers and crotchet rests) and plays it on one key in time with the metronome; the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) clap two quavers within one beat (“ti-ti”); (2) make the crotchet-rest gesture without losing the beat.

What you need: a computer with speakers, the board and, if available, a projector. It helps to have done Lesson 5 first. No music knowledge needed: the keyboard does the work; you guide.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise the keyboard. In class your attention needs to be on the children, not on the buttons. Repeat these steps until you can do them without looking at the guide:

  • Open the Cosmic Keyboard and the metronome (pulse, about 60 BPM with tap, start and click, as in Lesson 1).
  • With the metronome running, play Z twice within the same click: once with the click and once halfway to the next. That is “ti-ti”, two quavers. Then let a whole click go by without playing: that is a crotchet rest.
  • Play the two rhythms in this guide, saying the syllables out loud: “ta”, “ti-ti” and a soft “sh” for the rest.
  • Look at the pupil’s worksheet at the end: you can project it or print one for each child.

Before we start: the chocolate bar in eight

Ti-ti: two quavers in one beat

If the crotchet is a quarter of the chocolate bar, breaking it in half again gives us quavers: two in each beat. We say “ti-ti” and write them with a flag or, when they come in twos, joined by a beam.

Four crotchets or eight quavers: the same chocolate bar.

Two quavers joined by a beam fit into one beat: “ti-ti”.

The rest counts too

Rests fill the silent parts of the music. When the music goes quiet, the beat carries on: every note value has its own rest, which lasts just as long. A crotchet rest lasts one beat, a minim rest two, a semibreve rest four.

The semibreve rest (hangs from line 4), the minim rest (sits on line 3), the crotchet rest and the quaver rest.

Did you know…? The semibreve rest and the minim rest look alike: two small black rectangles. The semibreve rest hangs from line 4 (it is heavier, it lasts longer) and the minim rest sits on line 3.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Last week we broke the chocolate bar into four. Today we are going to break it into eight, and we are going to learn to count the silence. At the end, every pair will read a rhythm from the board and play it on the keyboard.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can read and play “ti-ti” and rests.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the chocolate bar in four

    With the metronome set slowly, you clap crotchets (“ta”), minims (“ta-a”) and a semibreve (“ta-a-a-a”), counting the clicks; the class follows you.

    “What happens if we break the crotchet in half? How many pieces fit into one click?”

  3. 17 min

    New material: ti-ti and the rest

    1. Ti-ti (8 min). Clap four beats saying “ta, ta, ta, ta”; the class repeats. Then “ta, ta, ti-ti, ta”: the two claps of “ti-ti” are quick, within the same click. Repeat with words: “sun, sun, ap-ple, sun”. First the whole class; then one half keeps the beat with their feet and the other half claps the rhythm, and then they swap. Show the chocolate bar broken into eight.
    2. The rest you count (9 min). Clap “ta, ta, ta, —”: on the fourth beat, move your hands apart without a sound, as if you were opening a door. That gesture is the crotchet rest. The class repeats it. Write the rests on the board and the class copies them. Game: you point to a note value or a rest and the class claps or makes the gesture; first everyone, then row by row.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the drawings on the screen; without a screen, copy them onto the board.

  4. 20 min

    Application: reading and playing a rhythm

    Reading (8 min). Write the two rhythms below on the board. You read the first one with syllables and clap it; then the whole class reads it. The pairs read the second one, facing each other, and then the whole class claps it.

    The two rhythms for the application: you read the first one, the pairs read the second.

    Playing (12 min). Show it yourself first: with the metronome running, play the first rhythm on Z while you say the syllables.

    1. A pair comes to the computer: one says the syllables, pointing to each note value, and the other plays.
    2. The class says the syllables quietly and gives a thumbs-up if the written rhythm was heard.
    3. Guessing game: the pair chooses one of the two rhythms without saying which and plays it; the class guesses which one it was.
    4. The pairs take turns and swap roles.

    A rhythm in three beats (if there is time left). Clap a rhythm of three beats counting “one, two, three”, with a strong clap on “one”, like a waltz or a Colombian bambuco. The class repeats it. There is no need to explain the time signature: that comes in Lesson 7. In the pupil’s workbook, My Little Music Grammar · Level 1 (chapter 6, exercise 3), the pupils read a rhythm like this.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Write one last four-beat rhythm with a “ti-ti” and a rest; the pairs who have not had a turn yet read it and play it, and the class gives a thumbs-up if it matches.

    “How many quavers fit into one beat? What happens to the beat when there is a rest?”

    Listen to the answers: two quavers in one beat, and the beat carries on even when nothing sounds.

Challenge for fast finishers: “Write a four-beat rhythm with a minim, a ti-ti and a crotchet rest. How many different rhythms can you make by changing the order?” Answer: 6 (minim · ti-ti · rest, minim · rest · ti-ti, ti-ti · minim · rest, ti-ti · rest · minim, rest · minim · ti-ti, rest · ti-ti · minim). In Level 2 they will do the same sum with the oldest song in the world.

With the Melody Machine: ti-ti

To close the lesson, or to start the next one. Open the Melody Machine and press SPIN.

  1. Quaver hunt. The class looks on the screen for two notes joined by a beam: they are two quavers, a “ti-ti” in one beat.
  2. Say the rhythm. Press PLAY and have the class say the rhythm with syllables: “ta” for each crotchet, “ti-ti” for each pair of quavers.
  3. Spin again. With HOLD, keep the bar with the most quavers and spin the others.

The machine does not write rests: after singing the melody, the class can replace a crotchet with a rest and sing it again, staying silent on that beat.

If they find it hard:
  • The ti-ti comes out uneven or slow: use words with two equal syllables (“ta-ble”, “ap-ple”) and slow the metronome down with tap.
  • They clap during the rest: exaggerate the door-opening gesture and say “sh” quietly; the rest has its own sound in the mouth too.
  • They lose the beat after the rest: have them keep the beat with their foot the whole time, even when their hands are silent.
  • They mix up the semibreve rest and the minim rest: “the semibreve is heavier and hangs; the minim is light and sits down”.

In this lesson one key is enough, Z: what we are learning is the rhythm, not the note.

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs read and play the rhythm on the board with its “ti-ti”s and its rests, and the class confirms it with a thumbs-up. In addition, most pupils clap two quavers within one beat and make the rest gesture without losing the beat.

These lessons are a starting point. Each teacher can design the examples to suit their own class. For our part, we will keep adding lesson topics according to the requests we receive through the contact form.

Ti-ti and the rest, at home

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we learned that two quavers (“ti-ti”) fit into one beat and that the rest counts too. This week, play together:

  1. Open the keyboard on the computer: musicmotionsuite.com/apps/mmk-demo.html
  2. Walk the rhythm: one person claps a calm beat and says “ta”, “ti-ti” or “sh”. The other walks: one step for “ta”, two quick little steps for “ti-ti”, and still as a statue for “sh”. Then swap.
  3. Words that sound: look for words that sound like “ta” (sun, cat, dog) and others like “ti-ti” (apple, tiger, table). Make up a four-beat rhythm with them and a rest.
  4. On the keyboard: the child plays the rhythm you made up on one key while the adult says the words.
  5. The Melody Machine: open musicmotionsuite.com/suite/tragamonedas.html, press SPIN and look for the “ti-ti”s (two notes joined by a beam). Press PLAY and say the rhythm with syllables. With PRINT you can print the melody. No registration needed.

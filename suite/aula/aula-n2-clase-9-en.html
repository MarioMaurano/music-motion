In the classroom — Dynamics and articulation

Dynamics and articulation

Level 2 · Lesson 9 · loud and soft, crescendo and diminuendo, staccato, accent, tenuto, pause and the two curved lines · Beethoven and the “Ode an die Freude”

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair writes dynamics and articulation marks into four bars of the “Ode an die Freude” (Ode to Joy) and plays them on the Keyboard; the class guesses which signs they used.

Secondary objectives: (1) put the dynamics in order from pp to ff; (2) tell a tie from a slur; (3) sight-sing a familiar melody, reading the names, singing and following dynamics and articulation all at the same time.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, the Cosmic Keyboard, the two versions of the Ode in the Score (the usual one and the one for this lesson, with dynamics and articulation), the board and, if available, a projector.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Listen to the two versions of the Ode, one after the other, following the score. The notes are the same; what changes is how they are played.
  • On the Keyboard, play the Ode on the middle row: D D F G · G F D S · A A S D · D S S.
  • Try it in two ways: letting go of each key straight away (staccato) and keeping it pressed until the next one (legato). Then turn the VOL knob while you play: drag it up or down, or use the mouse wheel.

Before we start: the same notes, different music

Dynamics: how loud it sounds

Dynamics tell you how loudly the music sounds. They are written under the stave with Italian letters: p (piano) is soft and f (forte) is loud. With m (mezzo, “half”) and by repeating the letters, you build a staircase with eight steps:

softest possible
pianissimo
piano
mezzo-piano
mezzo-forte
forte
fortissimo
loudest possible

Some dynamics change all of a sudden. The sforzando (sf or sfz) is a strong accent on a single note, and then it goes straight back to the dynamic that was there before. The fp (forte-piano) starts loud and drops straight away to piano.

Crescendo and diminuendo: changing little by little

To get louder or softer little by little, you use hairpins, two lines that open out or close up. The one that opens is the crescendo (cresc.): louder and louder. The one that closes is the diminuendo (dim.), also called decrescendo: softer and softer. Sometimes the word is written instead of the drawing.

From p up to f, and from f back down again.

Articulation: how each note starts and ends

Articulation marks are small signs next to the note head. They tell you how the note starts and how long it really lasts.

The same note, four ways of playing it.

  • Staccato (a dot): the note is cut short; it sounds for less than its value and leaves some air before the next one.
  • Accent (>): the note starts louder than the ones around it.
  • Tenuto (a little dash): the note is held for its whole value, nice and full.
  • Pause, or fermata (an arc with a dot): the note stays as long as the musician wants; the music waits.

Two curved lines that look alike

Both are a curved line, but they say different things. The tie joins two notes that are the same: the second one is not played, it is added to the first. The slur covers different notes: they are played smoothly joined, without breaking the sound between them (legato); on the violin, in one bow, and for the voice, in one breath.

Tie: G minim + G crotchet = one single G lasting three beats.

Slur: E, F, G and A, joined.

The clue: if the two notes have the same name and the line joins them head to head, it is a tie.

The time machine: Beethoven and the Ninth

Vienna, 7 May 1824. Ludwig van Beethoven gives the first performance of his Ninth Symphony. For years he has been almost completely deaf; he hears the music inside his head. At the end, he cannot hear the applause, and one of the singers turns him round so that he can see the audience on its feet. In the last movement, a choir sings the “Ode an die Freude” (Ode to Joy), which today is the anthem of the European Union.

Beethoven wrote his dynamics very carefully and loved contrasts: from pp to ff all of a sudden, and sforzandos on notes nobody expected. With him, dynamics stop being decoration: they are part of the music.

In the Score, the Ode for this lesson has these signs (the version from Level 1, Lesson 1, the usual one, has none):

Bars 1 to 4: piano and legato; a crescendo towards the forte; a diminuendo at the end of the phrase.

Bars 5 to 8: piano again, now staccato; a strong accent, like a sforzando, on the G; mezzo-forte with accents and a crescendo; forte, tenuto and a pause at the end.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “A score tells us which notes to play. But it also tells us whether to play them soft or loud, short or long. Today we are going to learn those signs and use them.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can read and play dynamics and articulation.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: two versions of the same melody

    Sing the beginning of the Ode with the note names yourself, and the class repeats it. Then play the usual Ode and straight after it the one for this lesson. Ask: “What has changed, if the notes are the same?” Write on the board what they say: louder, softer, short notes, a long note at the end.

  3. 20 min

    New material: the signs

    1. The dynamics staircase (4 min). Write p and f on the board yourself. Say a sentence very softly and then very loudly; the class repeats it. Complete the staircase from pp to ff: the class says “Ode” on each step, from the bottom up. Explain sfz and fp with a clap: one loud clap, and soft straight away.
    2. Sight-singing with dynamics (5 min). Draw a large stave on the board and write on it a melody the class already knows by heart, for example the beginning of “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star”: C C G G A A G · F F E E D D C, with the names under the notes. As they already know the melody, the difficulty is not in the notes but in the dynamics. Under the notes, put a p or an f. First the class says the names in time with the beat, softly with p and loudly with f; then they sing them with the melody, following the same signs. Change the signs without warning: p in the first half and f in the second, a single f in the middle, then mp and mf. When it is going well, a pupil comes up to the board and is the one who changes the signs.
    3. The hairpins (3 min). Draw a crescendo yourself: the class sings E, E, F, G, getting louder. Then a diminuendo, getting softer.
    4. Articulation (3 min). Sing G four times yourself, with each articulation: staccato (short), accent (loud at the start), tenuto (full) and pause (until you lower your hand). The class imitates you, and then you point to a sign and the class sings it.
    5. Sight-singing, all of it (2 min). Go back to “Twinkle, Twinkle” on the board and add, here and there, a dot above a note (short) or a dash (long), as well as the dynamics. Now the class reads the names, sings the melody and follows the dynamics and the articulation, all at the same time. That is sight-singing: eyes, ears and attention working together. Tell the class when they manage it: it is a goal they can already reach, and it shows.
    6. The two curved lines (3 min). Draw both. With the tie, the class sings G and holds it for three beats, without singing it again. With the slur, they sing E, F, G, A in one breath. “What is the difference between them?”

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the score on the screen with ⛶ FULL; without a screen, copy the signs onto the board.

  4. 17 min

    Application: each pair’s Ode

    Writing (6 min). On the pupil’s sheet each pair gets bars 5 to 8 of the Ode, with no signs, and writes in at least one dynamic, one hairpin and two articulation marks.

    Playing (9 min). First play your own version on the Keyboard yourself: you play the melody on the middle row and a pupil turns the VOL knob for the dynamics and the hairpins. Staccato means letting go of the key straight away; legato, keeping it pressed until the next one; the pause, holding the last one. Then the pairs take turns: one plays and the other works VOL, without showing the sheet. The class guesses which signs they wrote, and the pair shows the sheet.

    Recording (2 min). With the pair that worked best together, record their version on the Keyboard (REC, the ⏺ button) and listen to it together with ▶.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Play the Ode with dynamics again: the class puts a hand up every time the music changes its dynamic.

    “Which is softer, p or mp? What does a dot above a note do? Which curved line joins two notes that are the same?”

    Listen to the answers: p; it makes it short; the tie.

Challenge for the quick ones: “The computer keyboard doesn’t know how hard you press a key. Which instrument does know, and gets its name from it?” The piano, which was first called the pianoforte: the first keyboard instrument that could play soft and loud depending on the finger. And one more: “A bar of 4/4 has a minim tied to a crotchet and then a rest. How many beats does the sound last?” Three.
If they find it hard:
  • They mix up p and f: “p” for peaceful, “f” for full force; the Italian word comes afterwards.
  • Their crescendo comes out all at once: have them go up one step per note, counting out loud.
  • They don’t see the difference between the curved lines: have them read the names of the two notes; if they are the same, it is a tie.
  • On the Keyboard, the one working VOL gets ahead: have them follow the score with a finger and turn the knob only when the hairpin arrives.

In the workbook: chapter 9

The pupil’s workbook covers this lesson in four pages: dynamics (with sf and fp), articulation, the two curved lines and a sight-singing page: “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” with two little boxes so that each pupil writes their own dynamic and sings it following their signs, as in “Sight-singing with dynamics” in this guide. The articulation and curved-line pages show the same bars of the Ode with dynamics as this guide. Canelo explains articulation in his own way: a short bark is staccato, howling at the moon is tenuto, and sitting perfectly still waiting for a biscuit is a pause.

After the chapter come two pages, “Forte and piano: loud and soft all over the world”: the hand drum of the Indigenous peoples of North America and the djembe from West Africa, the difference between intensity (the height of the wave, how far the skin of the drum moves) and volume (how the ear feels it), and dynamics games with palms on the table and with the VOL knob on the Keyboard. It is a good place to remind them that the same note can sound soft or loud without changing its pitch.

The Ode is played on the middle row, with five keys: A C · S D · D E · F F · G G. Dynamics are made with the VOL knob; articulation, with how long the key stays pressed.

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs write a dynamic, a hairpin and two articulation marks in the right place, and the class guesses at least half of the signs in each version played. In addition, most pupils put the six dynamics in the right order from pp to ff and tell the two curved lines apart on the sheet.

Soft, loud, short, long

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we learnt the signs that tell you how to play the notes: soft or loud, short or long. This week, play together:

  1. Two Odes: listen to the “Ode an die Freude” in the Score, first without signs (the usual version) and then with them (the version with dynamics). What has changed?
  2. The conductor: on the keyboard (musicmotionsuite.com/apps/mmk-demo.html), one of you plays the Ode with the keys D D F G, G F D S, A A S D, D S S, and the other turns the VOL knob like a conductor: softer, louder.
  3. Sight-singing with dynamics: write the note names of “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” on a piece of paper (C C G G A A G · F F E E D D C) and underneath, here and there, a p or an f. The child reads them out loud, softly with p and loudly with f. Then change the signs, and swap roles.
  4. Guess the sign: sing a song you know, short and soft, or long and loud; the other person says which sign you used.

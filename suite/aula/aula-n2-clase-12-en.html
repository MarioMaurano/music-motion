In the classroom — The premiere

Premiere: “I’ve Lost My Sandwich!”

Level 2 · Lesson 12 · the class premieres its opera: the narrator, the five scenes, the interludes and the grand finale · the whole work is recorded

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class, in the five groups from Lesson 11 and each pupil with a job. Length: 50 minutes. If the premiere is for families, hold the dress rehearsal in one lesson and the premiere in another.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, the whole opera is performed, from the overture to the grand finale, without stopping, and it is recorded.

Secondary objectives: (1) every pupil does their job in the premiere; (2) while listening to the recording, each group says why its music tells what happens in its scene: major or minor, dynamics, tempo.

What you need: the classroom computer with speakers and a projector; the music for the five scenes, saved in the Score in Lesson 11; the Cosmic Keyboard; for the programme, the Seikilos song with chords, Rocinante’s gallop from Telemann’s suite “Burlesque de Quixotte” (in Downloads) and “Ode an die Freude” (Ode to Joy) with dynamics; a phone to record with; and the printed programme from the workbook (chapter 12) or a copy of the sheet in this guide.

Before the lesson (15 minutes, at home): open the five scenes in the Score and listen to them in order; read the libretto in this guide aloud with a stopwatch (the whole opera lasts about 15 minutes); and decide where each group will stand, so that nobody has to cross the room between one scene and the next.

Before we start: the libretto

I’ve Lost My Sandwich! · an opera in five scenes

The narrator reads their part before each scene. Then the group says its dialogue (the one it wrote in Lesson 11) and plays or performs its music. If a character speaks while the music is playing, it is a melodrama.

Scene 1 · Break time (cheerful · C major)
Narrator: “The bell rings for break. The children run out into the playground, jumping, playing ball and laughing. Lucy is hungry: this morning her grandma made her favourite sandwich.”

Scene 2 · Where is it? (restless · D minor)
Narrator: “Lucy opens her school bag… and the sandwich isn’t there. She looks between her exercise books, in her pockets, under the bench. Her friends help her look. Who could have taken it?”

Scene 3 · The suspicious dog (playful · G major)
Narrator: “At the playground gate appears Canelo, the school dog. He wags his tail, sniffs the school bags and licks his lips. Everyone looks at him. Could it be him? Canelo runs round in circles, jumps and hides behind the teacher.”

Scene 4 · No sandwich (sad · A minor)
Narrator: “Break is over. Lucy sits alone on a step. She is hungry and sad. Canelo comes over slowly and rests his head on her knees.”

Scene 5 · Here it is! (happy · C major)
Narrator: “Back in the music room, Lucy opens the piano lid… and there is her sandwich! She had left it there at the morning rehearsal. It wasn’t Canelo’s fault: he could only smell the sandwich in the school bag. Lucy shares it with her friends, and Canelo can breathe easy at last.”

The libretto is a starting point: each group can change the words of its scene, and the narrator can be one pupil or several, one per scene.

The premiere programme

The premiere brings together the work of the year. It is the same printed programme as in the workbook; each school puts it together to suit itself.

  1. Overture. The Seikilos song with chords: the class “kithara” (Lessons 7 and 10).
  2. The opera. Scenes 1 to 5, each with its narration, its dialogue and its music.
  3. Dance interlude. Rocinante’s gallop, from Telemann’s suite about Don Quixote (in Downloads): the dancers gallop, a long step and a short step.
  4. Percussion interlude. The drum circle (Lesson 9): the whole class taps the table with their palms, to the same beat, and grows from pp to fff following the signs.
  5. Grand finale. Everyone sings “Ode to Joy” with dynamics (Lesson 9).

The jobs

An opera is made by lots of people working together. Every pupil has a job, as well as their group:

JobWhat they do
NarratorsRead the libretto and introduce each scene.
LibrettistsWrite the dialogues and fill in the printed programme.
Set designersPaint the backdrop for each scene or project the drawings.
CostumesPrepare the clothes and the props: the school bag and the sandwich.
DancersDance in the interlude and in the finale.
ChoirSings the grand finale.
Suite techniciansProject the score with the Score (FULL), play live on the Keyboard and play the music for each scene.
Did you know…? In opera many arts work together: literature tells the story, theatre acts it out, dance moves it, painting and set design give it its world and its colours. And music brings them all together: that is why opera is sometimes called “the total work of art”.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Today is the premiere. You wrote this opera yourselves: the story, the dialogues and the music. Today we perform the whole thing, as in a theatre, and we record it.”

    Write the goal on the board: We can perform our opera from beginning to end.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: each scene and its key

    Ask each group, in the order of the story: “Which is your scene? Major or minor? Why?” Each group answers in one sentence. Play the home note of each scene yourself on the Keyboard (Tonal, the A key) so that the technicians have it in their ears.

  3. 10 min

    New material: the dress rehearsal

    1. The order (3 min). Write the programme on the board. Each job says what it does and when it comes in.
    2. The entries (5 min). Run through only the beginnings and endings: the narrator reads the first sentence of each scene, the group says the first line of the dialogue and the technicians play the first two bars. That way everyone knows when it is their turn.
    3. Silence and applause (2 min). Practise with the class yourself: before the overture, total silence; at the end of each number, a short round of applause; at the very end, the big applause and the bow.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the technicians show the score of each scene in the Score (FULL) while it plays; without a screen, the programme written on the board is enough.

  4. 22 min

    Application: the premiere

    Start recording with the phone and don’t stop: if something goes wrong, carry on, just as in a real theatre. The overture, the five scenes, the dance interlude, the percussion interlude and the grand finale, with everyone standing to sing the Ode.

  5. 10 min

    Reviewing the goal: listening to the recording

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Listen together to part of the recording, for example scenes 3 and 4. “What changed from one scene to the next? How did the music do it?” Listen to the answers: from major to minor, from fast to slow, from staccato to legato, from f to p. Finish with a question for each group: “What would you change in your scene for the next performance?”

In the workbook: chapter 12, “The premiere”

The last two pages of chapter 12 of the pupil’s workbook, My Little Music Grammar · Level 2, prepare this lesson. “The premiere: a show by everyone” has the table of jobs, with a column to write down who does each one, and a space to draw the backdrop for the scene or the character’s costume. “The premiere programme” is the printed programme, with the date and the names of the narrators and technicians to fill in, and the “Did you know…?” about the total work of art.

If they find it hard:
  • There are nerves: have the group say the dialogue once quietly before starting; the narrator can also read the dialogue if someone freezes.
  • A piece of music isn’t ready: the group plays its scene live on the Keyboard (Tonal, with the KEY and MODE of its scene), even if it is only four bars.
  • They get lost between scenes: have the narrator hold the programme and say the number before each part.
  • There isn’t time for everything: drop an interlude; the five scenes and the grand finale are never dropped.
Challenge for the quick ones: “Compose four bars of a new overture with the melody machine, in C major, to play while the audience sits down.” And one more: “Write a review of the premiere for the school newsletter: which scene you liked best and what the music did in it.”
How to tell it worked: the whole opera is performed, from the overture to the grand finale, and it is recorded; every pupil did their job; and while listening to the recording each group explains in one sentence what its music does to tell its scene.

These lessons are a starting point. Each teacher can design the examples to suit their own class. For our part, we will keep adding lesson topics according to the requests we receive through the contact form.

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today the class premiered the opera it composed: a narrator, five scenes with their own music, a dance, a drum circle and a grand finale sung by everyone. This week, at home:

  1. The premiere: ask your child to tell you the story of Lucy and her sandwich, and what job they had.
  2. Your child’s scene: ask them to play you the music of their scene on the keyboard (musicmotionsuite.com/apps/mmk-demo.html) and tell you why it is major or minor.
  3. The grand finale: open “Ode to Joy” with dynamics and sing it together, from soft to loud.

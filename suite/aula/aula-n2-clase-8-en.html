In the classroom — Keys

Keys

Level 2 · Lesson 8 · the key signature, the two tricks, the relative minor and the leading note · the Baroque with Bach’s 15 Sinfonias

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair looks at four key signatures and names the major key of each one using the two tricks; the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) name the relative minor of a major key; (2) play the leading note and the tonic on the Keyboard, and say which of the two is the leading note.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, the Cosmic Keyboard, the Score demos in the table in this guide (the class already knows them all), the board and, if available, a projector. It helps to have done Lessons 4, 5 and 6.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Learn the order of the sharps by heart: F, C, G, D, A, E, B. The order of the flats is the same the other way round: B, E, A, D, G, C, F.
  • Try out the two tricks with the key signatures on the pupil’s sheet.
  • On the Keyboard, press Tonal (SYSTEM), C (KEY) and M (MODE). On the middle row, A is the tonic, C; J is the leading note, B; K is the high C. Play J and let your hand go by itself to K.

Before we start: the key to the key

Why key signatures exist

The major scale always has the same shape: tone, tone, diatonic semitone, tone, tone, tone, diatonic semitone (on the piano, a semitone; Lesson 4). Starting from C it works by itself, on the white keys. Starting from any other note, you need sharps or flats to keep that shape. Starting from D you need two: F♯ and C♯.

The major scale from D, with the sharps written on each note.

Instead of writing the sharp every time, you write it just once at the beginning of each stave, after the clef: that is the key signature. It applies to all the notes with that name, in every octave, until the end of the piece or until the key signature changes. It tells you which key the music is in, that is, which note is home (the tonic) and which scale is used.

The same scale with a key signature: F♯ and C♯ at the beginning, and the notes without sharps.

The Keyboard in Tonal does the same as the key signature: with KEY on D, the D key on the middle row, the third degree, already plays F♯ without anyone asking.

The order of the sharps and flats, and the two tricks

The sharps and flats of the key signature always appear in the same order and in the same place on the stave.

The sharps: F, C, G, D, A, E, B. Each one a fifth higher than the one before.

The flats: B, E, A, D, G, C, F. The same order the other way round; each one a fourth higher than the one before.

The sharps trick: the last sharp is the seventh degree, the leading note; the tonic is a diatonic semitone higher. Three sharps: the last one is G♯, so the key is A major.

The flats trick: the last flat but one is the name of the key. Three flats: B♭, E♭, A♭; the last but one is E♭, so the key is E♭ major. With only one flat there is no last but one: you have to remember it, it is F major. With no sharps or flats, C major.

F♯, C♯, G♯: a diatonic semitone above G♯ is A. A major.

B♭, E♭, A♭: the last but one is E♭. E♭ major.

The two tricks come from the slide “Key signature” in Elements of Music.

The relative minor and the leading note

Each key signature works for two keys: a major key and its relative minor. The relative minor uses the same notes, but starts on the sixth degree of the major, a minor third below the tonic (Lesson 6). C major and A minor have no sharps or flats; B♭ major and G minor have two flats.

C major: home is C.

A minor, its relative: the same notes, home is A, the sixth degree of C.

How can you tell which of the two it is? Look at where the melody starts and, above all, where it ends, and look for the leading note. The leading note is the seventh degree: the note that lies a diatonic semitone below the tonic and pushes towards it. In major it is already in the scale: in C major it is B. In minor, the scale has it a tone lower, and the music raises it by a chromatic semitone: in A minor, G becomes G♯. That sharp is not in the key signature: it appears next to the notes, and it is the clue that the key is minor.

C major: B, the leading note, rises to C.

A minor: G♯, the leading note, rises to A. The beginning of Mozart’s “Alla Turca” circles round exactly there: B, A, G♯, A.

In the tonal scale (the voice, the violin, the Keyboard in Tonal), the diatonic semitone (256/243) is smaller than the chromatic one (2187/2048): the leading note sits closer to the tonic and that is why it “pulls” harder towards it. On the piano the two semitones are the same.

The time machine: Bach and his 15 Sinfonias

Germany, 1723. Johann Sebastian Bach makes a fair copy of fifteen three-part keyboard pieces, the Sinfonias, for his pupils. Each one is in a different key, from the bottom up: C major, C minor, D major, D minor, E♭ major, E major, E minor, F major, F minor, G major, G minor, A major, A minor, B♭ major and B minor.

None of them has more than four sharps or flats. On many keyboards of the time, tuned for the closely related keys, the keys with many sharps or flats sounded out of tune. With the “well-tempered” tunings, such as Werckmeister’s, they could all be played, and Bach had already shown this in 1722 with The Well-Tempered Clavier: a prelude and a fugue in each of the 24 keys, the 12 major and the 12 minor. Around 1742 he wrote a second book, again with all 24: the two books together have 48 preludes and fugues.

The class already knows Sinfonia No. 9 (Lessons 4 and 5). Its key signature has four flats: A♭ major or F minor. It ends on F and E♮ appears, the leading note: it is F minor. The 15 Sinfonias, in three tunings, are in the demos .zip.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “At the beginning of every score there are some sharps or flats that look like decoration. Today we are going to learn to read them: they tell us where the home of the music is.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can read a key signature and say its key.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the major scale

    Sing the scale from C to C with the note names yourself, and the class repeats it. Ask: “Where are the two diatonic semitones?” Between E and F, and between B and C (on the piano, semitones). Write T T S T T T S on the board.

  3. 20 min

    New material: the key signature, the tricks and the relative minor

    1. The scale from D (5 min). On the board, write the scale from D yourself without sharps and count the steps with the class. “Where does the shape break?” Between E and F, and between B and C. Add the F♯ and the C♯. Then rub them out and write them just once at the beginning: the key signature. On the Keyboard, with Tonal and KEY D, play the middle row from A to K: you hear the scale of D, with F♯ and C♯.
    2. The order (5 min). The class says the order of the sharps with you several times, like a tongue-twister: F, C, G, D, A, E, B. Then backwards, and that is the order of the flats.
    3. The two tricks (5 min). Draw three sharps yourself and apply the trick out loud: “The last one is G♯; a diatonic semitone higher, A: A major.” The same with three flats: “The last but one is E♭: E♭ major.” The class tries with two sharps and with two flats.
    4. The relative minor and the leading note (5 min). On the Keyboard (Tonal, KEY C, M), play J yourself and stop: the class sings the missing note, C. Press m (MODE) and KEY A: the same row plays the scale of A minor. Explain that the relative minor starts on the sixth degree and that in minor the leading note is raised: G♯. Listen together to the beginning of “Alla Turca”.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the key signatures on the screen; without a screen, draw them on the board.

  4. 17 min

    Application: what key is it in?

    The pieces they know (8 min). Open the pieces in the table in the Score, one at a time. The class reads the key signature, applies the trick and says the major key. With “Alla Turca” and the Bach Sinfonia, ask: “Major, or its relative minor?”, and listen together to the ending.

    The pupil’s sheet (6 min). For each key signature on the sheet, each pair writes the major key and its relative minor.

    The leading note on the keyboard (3 min). Two or three pairs come up to the Keyboard: one plays the leading note (J) and the other resolves it to the tonic (K). They change the KEY and try again.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Draw four key signatures yourself, one at a time, and the class says the key all together.

    “What is the sharps trick? And the flats trick? Where does the relative minor start? Which note pushes towards home?”

    Listen to the answers: the last sharp is the leading note; the last flat but one is the key; on the sixth degree; the leading note.

Challenge for the quick ones: “On the Keyboard, the KEY buttons are in this order: F, C, G, D, A, E, B. Which famous order is that?” It is the order of the sharps. “And if you read them from right to left?” B, E, A, D, G, C, F: the order of the flats. And one more: “Which major key has five sharps?” The last one is A♯; a diatonic semitone higher, B major.
If they find it hard:
  • They can’t remember the order: a sentence made from the initials helps; let the class invent its own for F, C, G, D, A, E, B.
  • With sharps they say the note of the last sharp: remind them that the last one is the leading note, not home; home is one step higher.
  • With one flat they look for the last but one: one flat is F major, learnt by heart.
  • They mix up the relative minor: have them count six notes up from the major tonic (C, D, E, F, G, A), or a third down (C, B, A).

In the workbook: chapter 8 and context

The pupil’s workbook covers this lesson in four pages: the key signature, the two tricks, the relative minor and the leading note, and a game of “Key signature detectives” with the Ode, “Alla Turca”, “Ave Maria” and Bach’s Sinfonia No. 9, the same pieces as in the table in this guide.

On the leading-note page, Canelo gets confused: if the piano only has semitones, why does the piano teacher talk about a “diatonic semitone”? And he answers his own question: she is talking about what is written (G♯ → A changes its name, so it is diatonic), but her fingers play a semitone. The violinist, on the other hand, really plays it: his G♯ sits closer to A and pushes harder. It is a good moment to tell the class that the same word depends on the context: in the score, chromatic and diatonic; on the piano, a semitone; on the violin, the voice or the Keyboard in Tonal, two semitones of different sizes that you can hear.

The key signature in the Harmony Machine. The workbook invites the class to use the Harmony Machine. Press SPIN and then change the KEY without changing the melody: G, D, A, E. The same melody moves house and the sharps of the key signature appear one by one, in the order the class learnt. Then press KEY C with M (C major) and KEY A with m (A minor): neither of them has a key signature, but in A minor the G♯ appears in the notes: the leading note. The notes of each reel and the key (“Chorale in A minor”) can be read underneath, in The skeleton.

This is how the keys sound in Axiomatic. In Tonal, each row plays the scale of the key chosen with KEY and MODE: on the middle row, A is the tonic (I), J the leading note (VII) and K the high tonic.

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note. KEY D is D major, and KEY B♭ (B and ♭ in ALTERATION) is B♭ major.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs correctly name the major key of the four key signatures in the review; most pupils correctly write at least four of the six relative minors on the sheet (E, D, B, G, F♯, C minor); and the pairs who come up to the Keyboard resolve the leading note without help.

In the classroom — A piece and a half

A piece and a half

Level 2 · Lesson 2 · the dot and semiquavers · the Romantic period with Schubert’s “Ave Maria”

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair reads with syllables and plays on one key, in time with the metronome, a bar with a dotted crotchet and its quaver, four semiquavers and a crotchet; the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) explain with the chocolate bar that the dot adds half of the note’s value; (2) find the dotted crotchets of the voice in the score of the “Ave Maria”.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, the board, space to move and, if possible, a projector. It helps to have done Lesson 1. No music knowledge needed: the chocolate bar and the body do the sums.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Open the Cosmic Keyboard and the metronome (pulse, about 60 BPM with tap, start and click). Play on Z four notes inside a single click: “ti-ki-ti-ki”, four semiquavers. Then the gallop: one long note and one short one, “ta-i ti”, which together take up two clicks.
  • Open the “Ave Maria” in the Score and listen to the beginning of the voice (the top stave): you will see dotted crotchets followed by a quaver. With ⛶ FULL and − it is easier to see; with the ♩ PULSE knob you can make it slower.
  • Look at the pupil’s sheet at the end.

Before we start: the chocolate bar in sixteen and the dot

Ti-ki-ti-ki: the chocolate bar in sixteen

In Level 1 the chocolate bar went as far as eight pieces: the quavers. If each quaver is split in half again, you get semiquavers: four in each beat, sixteen in a semibreve. They are written with two beams and said “ti-ki-ti-ki”.

The same chocolate bar in four, in eight and in sixteen pieces.

Crotchets and groups of four semiquavers: “ta · ti-ki-ti-ki”.

The dot: a piece and a half

A dot after a note makes it longer by half of its value. The minim is worth 2; with a dot, 2 + 1 = 3 (they already saw it in the waltz in Level 1). The crotchet is worth 1; with a dot, 1 + ½ = 1½. The dotted crotchet is nearly always followed by a quaver, which completes the two beats: long-short, like a galloping horse.

Dotted minim (3 beats) and dotted crotchet with its quaver (2 beats).

The gallop: “ta-i ti, ta-i ti · ta-i ti, ta-a”.

The time machine: the Romantic period

Vienna, 1825. Franz Schubert sets a poem to music: a young girl, hidden in a cave, prays to the Virgin Mary. It is the “Ave Maria”, one of the most sung melodies in the world. It is Romantic music: what matters is what you feel; the melody sings like a voice and the piano wraps it in an accompaniment that never stops. In the voice there are dotted crotchets, which let it breathe, and in the piano, fast notes that sound like a harp.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Today the chocolate bar is going to be split into sixteen, and we are going to meet a little dot that makes notes longer. At the end, each pair will read and play a bar with all of that.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can read and play semiquavers and dotted notes.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the chocolate bar in eight

    Clap crotchets (“ta”) and quavers (“ti-ti”) with the metronome; the class follows you. Ask: “How many quavers fit in a semibreve?” 8. “And what if we split each quaver again?”

  3. 15 min

    New material: ti-ki-ti-ki and the gallop

    1. Ti-ki-ti-ki (6 min). Show the chocolate bar in sixteen. Say “ta, ti-ki-ti-ki, ta, ti-ki-ti-ki” yourself while marking the beat with your foot; the four syllables fit into a single step. The class repeats: first everyone, then one half marks the beat and the other says the syllables, and they swap. With words: “Sun, ca-ter-pil-lar”.
    2. The dot (4 min). Write a minim and add the little dot: “2 and half of 2: 3”. Then a crotchet: “1 and half of 1: one and a half”. With the chocolate bar: the dotted crotchet is a piece and a half.
    3. The gallop (5 min). Gallop on the spot yourself: long step, short step, “ta-i ti, ta-i ti”. The class gallops with you while you say the syllables. Then write the gallop on the board: dotted crotchet and quaver.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the drawings on the screen; without a screen, copy them onto the board.

  4. 22 min

    Application: reading, playing and listening

    The goal bar (12 min). Write the bar below on the board. Read it yourself with syllables and play it on Z with the metronome slow.

    The goal bar: dotted crotchet and quaver, four semiquavers and a crotchet. 1½ + ½ + 1 + 1 = 4.

    1. The whole class says it with syllables while you point.
    2. A pair comes up to the computer: one says the syllables while pointing and the other plays.
    3. The class gives a thumbs-up if it sounded as written. The pairs take turns and swap roles.

    The “Ave Maria” (10 min). Project the score with ⛶ FULL and press ▶. The class listens to the beginning with their eyes on the voice (the top stave) and raises a hand every time they see a dotted crotchet. Then they listen to the piano: “Which note values make those fast notes?” A question about the period: “What do you feel? Is it a march, like ‘Alla Turca’, or a prayer?” In the workbook, My Little Music Grammar · Level 2 (chapter 2, exercise 4), there are two passages of the voice written as rhythm: bars 5 and 6 (dotted minim and two quavers) and bar 10 (dotted crotchet, two semiquavers and four quavers). The pupils read them with syllables and circle the dotted notes.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. The pairs who have not had a turn yet read the goal bar with syllables, all at once.

    “How many semiquavers fit in one beat? What does the dot do? How much is a dotted crotchet worth?”

    Listen to the answers: four; it adds half; one and a half.

A challenge for the quick ones: “How many semiquavers fit in a dotted minim?” A dotted minim is worth 3 beats, and 4 fit in each beat: 3 × 4 = 12. And a harder one: “If the dot adds half, how much do two dots in a row add?” The second adds half of the first: a double-dotted crotchet is worth 1 + ½ + ¼ = 1¾.
If they find it hard:
  • The semiquavers speed up or get muddled: slow the metronome down with tap and say “ca-ter-pil-lar” on each beat.
  • They mistake the dot for an extra note: the dot does not sound on its own; it only makes the note last longer. Have them keep the key pressed down.
  • The gallop comes out even: exaggerate the long step and the short one; “the horse stays in the air on the long step”.
  • They cannot find the dots in the score: slow the piece down with the knob and point them out on the screen yourself the first few times.

In this lesson one key is enough, Z: what is new is the rhythm.

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs read and play the goal bar in time, and the class confirms it with a thumbs-up. In addition, most pupils can explain how much a dot adds and find dotted crotchets in the “Ave Maria”.

These lessons are a starting point. Each teacher can design the examples to suit their own class. For our part, we will keep adding lesson topics according to the requests we receive through the contact form.

The dot makes the note longer by half of its value: a piece, plus half a piece more. Minim (2) + dot (1) = 3. Crotchet (1) + dot (½) = 1½.

One semibreve = 2 minims = 4 crotchets = 8 quavers = 16 semiquavers.

Fill in:

1 crotchet = semiquavers
1 dotted minim = crotchets
dotted crotchet + quaver = beats
1 semibreve = semiquavers

The chocolate bar can be split even further: demisemiquavers (1/32) and hemidemisemiquavers (1/64), in the next lesson, with Bach.

A piece and a half, at home

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we split the chocolate bar into sixteen pieces (semiquavers, “ti-ki-ti-ki”) and met the dot, which makes a note longer by half of its value. This week, play together:

  1. The gallop: one person says “ta-i ti, ta-i ti” and the other gallops around the house, long step and short step. Then swap.
  2. Ca-ter-pil-lar: look for four-syllable words (caterpillar, watermelon, macaroni) and say them inside a single clap: they are four semiquavers.
  3. The “Ave Maria”: listen to it together (search for “Schubert Ave Maria”). How does it make you feel? Is it music for marching or for being calm?
  4. Something livelier: listen to the last movement (Presto) of Schubert’s Quartet in D minor: the strings gallop like horses!

In the classroom — The numbers in music

The numbers in music

Level 1 · Lesson 8 · the chocolate bar, adding up a bar and counting the beats

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 8 to 12, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair builds a bar of 4/4 with cards that adds up to 4 beats, using the note values they choose, and plays it on one key in time with the metronome; the class counts and confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) say how many minims, crotchets or quavers fit into a semibreve; (2) count the beats in a song by multiplying bars by beats.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, the board and, if available, a projector. One sheet of cut-out cards per pair (at the end of this guide) and scissors, or the cards drawn on paper. It helps to have done Lessons 5 to 7. This is not a maths lesson: the numbers come out of music the children already know.

Before the lesson (15 minutes, at home): prepare the cards and practise. In class your attention needs to be on the children, not on the buttons.

  • Print one sheet of cards per pair and cut them out, or ask the pupils to cut them out at the start (5 extra minutes).
  • Open the Cosmic Keyboard and the metronome (pulse, about 60 BPM with tap, start and click, as in Lesson 1). Play the example bar on Z: minim, crotchet and ti-ti (“ta-a, ta, ti-ti”).
  • Open “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” in the Score. With the ♩ PULSE knob set it to 60 bpm and then to 120: at 120 it lasts half as long. Leave both tabs open.

Before we start: music counts

What they already know, in numbers

Without saying so, in the earlier lessons the children have already been doing sums: they split the chocolate bar into halves, quarters and eighths (Lessons 5 and 6), and they grouped the beats in 2s, 3s and 4s (Lesson 7). This lesson brings all of that together: note values are fractions, a bar is an addition, a song is counted by multiplying and the tempo is a number. In English there is a happy coincidence: a bar of chocolate and a bar of music are both split into equal pieces. Say it to the class; the workbook says it too.

A bar is an addition

A bar of 4/4 holds 4 beats. It can be filled in many ways: all that matters is that it adds up to 4. A minim is worth 2, a crotchet 1, a ti-ti 1 (two halves) and a crotchet rest also 1, even though it makes no sound.

The example bar: minim, crotchet and ti-ti. 2 + 1 + ½ + ½ = 4.

Other ways of filling the same bar:

1 + 1 + 1 + 1

2 + 2

½ + ½ + 1 + 1 (rest) + 1

The tempo is a number too

The number in the tempo marking tells you how many beats there are in a minute (bpm). At 60 bpm there is one beat per second, like the second hand of a clock; at 120, two per second. You can see it with the ♩ PULSE knob: if the number doubles, the song lasts half as long, but it has the same number of beats.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Music is full of numbers, and you already use them without noticing. Today we are going to look for them. At the end, every pair will build a bar like a jigsaw puzzle and play it.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can build a bar that adds up to 4 and play it.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the chocolate bar

    Draw the chocolate bar whole, in halves, in quarters and in eighths. You ask and the class answers together:

    “How many minims fit into a semibreve? How many crotchets? And how many quavers?”

    Next to each row, write its number: 1, 2, 4, 8. “Each time we split it in half: twice as many pieces, and each piece lasts half as long.”

  3. 17 min

    New material: adding, multiplying and measuring

    1. A bar is an addition (8 min). Draw an empty bar of 4/4 with its four boxes. Fill it yourself first with the example: minim (2), crotchet (1), ti-ti (1). Add it up out loud: “2 + 1 + 1 = 4”. The class claps it: “ta-a, ta, ti-ti”. Then ask the whole class for another way to fill it, and another: four crotchets, two minims, a semibreve… Each time they clap it.
    2. Counting a song (5 min). Project “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star”. “How many bars does it have?” They already know: 12. “And how many beats are there in each bar?” 4. “How many beats are there in the whole song?” Count in fours on your fingers (4, 8, 12…) up to 48: that is 12 × 4. With the waltz from Lesson 7: 4 bars of 3 beats, 12 beats.
    3. The tempo is a number (4 min). With the ♩ PULSE knob, set “Twinkle, Twinkle” to 60 bpm: one beat per second. The class marks the beat while watching the second hand of a clock (or the clock on the computer). Then set it to 120: “What happened? How many beats does the song have now?” It still has 48; they just go twice as fast.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the score and the drawings on the screen; without a screen, copy them onto the board.

  4. 20 min

    Application: the bar jigsaw

    Hand out one sheet of cards per pair (at the end of this guide) and the empty bar. Show it yourself first: build a bar on the board that is different from the example, add it up out loud and play it on Z with the metronome.

    1. Each pair builds their bar with the cards, without going over 4 or falling short, and adds it up out loud.
    2. One pair comes to the computer: one says the syllables, pointing to each card, and the other plays.
    3. The class adds up with them and gives a thumbs-up if the bar makes 4 and sounded the way it was built.
    4. The pairs take turns and swap roles. Those who finish early build a waltz bar that adds up to 3.
  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. The pairs who have not had a turn yet show their bar from their seats and the class adds it up.

    “How many different ways did we find to fill a bar? What number must the sum always make? What happens to the beats in a song if we play it faster?”

    Listen to the answers: many ways, always 4 in a bar of 4/4, and the beats stay the same even when the tempo changes.

A challenge for the quick ones: “In how many ways can you fill a bar of 4/4 using only minims and crotchets?” Answer: 5 (crotchet-crotchet-crotchet-crotchet, minim-crotchet-crotchet, crotchet-minim-crotchet, crotchet-crotchet-minim, minim-minim). Remember that in Lesson 6 they found 6 different rhythms with three note values: in Level 2 they will do the same kind of counting with the oldest song in the world, and the number that comes out is enormous. In the workbook (chapter 8) the pupils read that, more than two hundred years ago, a game for composing waltzes by throwing two dice was published under Mozart’s name; with numbers, millions of different waltzes could be built. They will play it in Level 2.

With the Melody Machine: the numbers in the melody

To round off the lesson, or to begin the next one. Open the Melody Machine and press SPIN.

  1. The sum of each bar. On the screen, the class adds up the note values in a bar: crotchet 1, minim 2, semibreve 4, each quaver half a beat. It always makes 4.
  2. The tempo is a number. Set the Tempo to 60: one beat per second, like the second hand. Raise it to 120: two beats per second. How many seconds does the melody last at each tempo?
  3. Four times four. Four bars of four beats: how many beats are there in the whole melody?

Answers: at 60 it lasts 16 seconds and at 120, 8 seconds; the melody has 4 × 4 = 16 beats.

If they find it hard:
  • The sum doesn’t work out: lay the cards on the chocolate bar drawn in quarters; each quarter is one beat, and you can see whether there is too much or too little.
  • They count the ti-ti as 2: “they are two quavers, but each one is a half; together they are worth one beat, like a crotchet split in two”.
  • Multiplying is hard for them: count in fours on your fingers (4, 8, 12…), pointing to one bar at a time in the score.
  • They rush when they play: slow the metronome down with tap and say the syllables before playing.

In this lesson one key is enough, Z: what the children build is the rhythm of the bar.

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs build a bar that adds up to 4 and play it the way it is built, and the class confirms it with a thumbs-up. In addition, most pupils can say how many of each note value fit into a semibreve and can count the beats in “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star”.

These lessons are a starting point. Each teacher can design the examples to suit their own class. For our part, we will keep adding lesson topics according to the requests we receive through the contact form.

The numbers in music, at home

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we discovered that music is full of numbers: note values are pieces of a chocolate bar, a bar is an addition and a song is counted by multiplying. This week, play together:

  1. The jigsaw: with the cards from the lesson (or ones drawn on paper), one person builds a bar that adds up to 4 and the other claps it. Then swap. How many different ways can you find?
  2. The second hand: watch the second hand of a clock together and clap one beat per second: that is 60 bpm. Then try twice as fast: 120.
  3. Counting a song: sing “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star” and count the bars on your fingers: there are 12. How many beats are there in the whole song?
  4. The Melody Machine: open musicmotionsuite.com/suite/tragamonedas.html, press SPIN and add up the beats in each bar (crotchet 1, minim 2, semibreve 4, quaver a half). Does it always make 4? With PRINT you can print the melody. No registration needed.

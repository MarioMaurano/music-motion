In the classroom — The dice of music

The dice of music

Level 2 · Lesson 13 · why 7 is the king of the dice · how the machine accompanies each bar · Mozart’s game: composing with a die · Pythagoras and the music of the spheres

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class and then in groups of 3 or 4. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each group composes a 4-bar tune by rolling a die (Mozart’s game), plays it on the Keyboard and says how many different tunes the table can give (6 × 6 × 6 × 6 = 1296).

Secondary objectives: (1) explain with the two-dice table why 7 comes up more often than 2 or 12; (2) say, from the colour of the square, how a bar sounds in the dice machine: as usual, the bass jumps, the bass stays, or the surprise (hemiola).

What you need: two dice per group (one light, one dark), a computer with speakers and internet, the Cosmic Keyboard, the dice machine, the board and, if possible, a projector. It helps to have done Lessons 10 and 11 (the chords I, IV and V, and the opera dice).

Pupil’s workbook: My Little Music Grammar · Level 2: the appendix “The maths of music”, pages 56 to 59 (Pythagoras and worksheets 1 to 3). If the pupils don’t have the booklet, you can print those pages from the workbook PDF. Worksheets 4 and 5 (pages 60–61, “The numbers of sound”) are left for Lesson 13b.

If a button is not clear: the user guide explains each one with pictures. At the top it has a search box: type, for example, “pulse”, “print” or “wav”.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Open the dice machine, choose WALTZ and press SPIN and PLAY. Above each card you see the two dice for that bar. Find a bar marked ↔: that is the hemiola, the surprise.
  • On the Keyboard, press SYSTEM → Tonal. Roll a die four times and play the tune from the table on page 59: above each note is its key on the middle row.
  • Look for the hidden tunes in that table: the Ode starts in row 3 and “Twinkle, Twinkle” in row 4.

Before we start: dice, sums and a mountain

Pythagoras and the music of the spheres

About 2,500 years ago, on the island of Samos, Pythagoras discovered with a string that sounds that go well together are simple numbers: half the string gives the octave; two thirds, the fifth. He thought the whole universe was made of numbers and that the planets, as they turned, made music: the music of the spheres. The planets don’t sing, but there really are numbers inside music. This lesson looks for them in the dice.

Why 7 is the king

With two dice there are 6 × 6 = 36 pairs. In a 6-by-6 table (the white die chooses the row, the red one the column), each square holds the sum. Squares with the same sum make diagonals; the one for 7 is the longest: 1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1, six ways. 2 and 12 have only one.

If you count the squares for each sum and draw them as bars, you get a mountain: it rises one step at a time up to 7 and comes down again. In 36 rolls, 7 comes up about 6 times; 2 and 12, once.

How the machine plays: the colours

In each bar the machine rolls the two dice once. What matters is how far from 7 the sum landed (the distance d): a 5 and a 9 are both 2 steps away. The distance gives the colour of the square, and the colour says how that bar is accompanied:

  • Green (d = 0 or 1): as usual, “OOM-pa-pa”.
  • Gold (d = 2): the bass jumps, “OOM-oom-pa”.
  • Orange (d = 3): the bass stays, “OOM……pa”.
  • Wine red (d = 4 or 5, sums 2, 3, 11 and 12): the surprise, “OOM……OOM……”, the hemiola: the bar of three splits in two.

The table has 16 green, 8 gold, 6 orange and only 6 wine-red squares, in the corners. That is why it nearly always sounds “as usual”, and the surprise comes about once every 6 bars. A surprise in every bar would be no surprise at all.

Mozart’s game

Mozart’s dice game (1792) had 16 bars, each with 11 options, chosen with two dice. The workbook’s is shorter: 4 bars, 6 options and a single die. Each column of the table is a bar, written over its chord (I, IV, V and I again): whatever option comes up fits, and the last always comes home to C. With 6 options in each of the 4 bars you get 6 × 6 × 6 × 6 = 1296 tunes.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Can you compose music by rolling dice? Mozart did. Today we are going to find out which numbers are hidden in the dice and compose with them.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can compose a tune with a die and play it.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: I, IV and V

    Play the chords I, IV and V of C major on the Keyboard and go back to I: “Where do we arrive home?” Remember the opera dice from Lesson 11: one die chose each bar of their scene.

  3. 17 min

    New material: 7, the mountain and the colours

    1. Pythagoras (3 min). Show the picture on page 56 and tell the music of the spheres in two sentences.
    2. Which sum comes up most? (7 min). Each group rolls the two dice 10 times and writes down the sums. Gather them on the board with tally marks, from 2 to 12: the mountain appears. Then, with the 6-by-6 table on page 57, count the ways to make 7 (six) and 2 (one).
    3. The machine’s colours (7 min). With the table on page 58, explain the distance from 7 and the four colours; clap “OOM-pa-pa” and the hemiola. Open the dice machine on WALTZ, press SPIN and PLAY: the class watches the dice of each bar and raises a hand when the surprise (↔) comes.
  4. 20 min

    Application: Mozart’s game

    Composing (10 min). First do an example on the board: roll the die four times and copy from the table on page 59 the bar in each row. Then each group rolls four times, copies its four bars and practises them on the Keyboard (SYSTEM Tonal, the middle row).

    Playing (7 min). Each group plays its tune to the class. “Did it come home to C?” Always: the last column ends on the tonic.

    The hidden tunes (3 min). The class looks for the well-known tunes in the table: the Ode starts in row 3; “Twinkle, Twinkle”, in row 4; there is a third one.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board.

    “Why does 7 come up more often than 12? How many tunes can the table give? Why do they always sound right?”

    Listen for the answers: because it has six ways and 12 has one; 6 × 6 × 6 × 6 = 1296; because each bar is written over its chord and the last one comes home.

A challenge for the quick ones: “With the two dice, roll four more times and colour each bar, as on page 58: now your tune has its accompaniment too.” And the mathematicians’ shortcut: the distance from 7 is written d = | a + b − 7 |.

In the workbook: the appendix, pages 56 to 59

Page 56 opens with Pythagoras by the sea, with his string and the planets, and introduces the five worksheets. Worksheet 1 (p. 57) has the 36-square table, the mountain of sums and the line of distances from 7. Worksheet 2 (p. 58), the colour table, a 4-bar example and four bars to colour with real dice. Worksheet 3 (p. 59), the table of Mozart’s game, with the key above each note and the degree below, an example (2, 5, 1, 4) and the hidden tunes.

Worksheets 4 and 5 (pp. 60–61) go on with the numbers of sound: D as the centre, and the octave, the fifth and the comma with a calculator.

If they find it hard:
  • They can’t see the mountain with 10 rolls: put the whole class’s rolls together; with more rolls it shows better.
  • They mix up row and column: the white die goes down the rows; the red one goes across the columns.
  • They can’t find the keys: SYSTEM Tonal and the middle row; the key is written above each note.
  • They don’t hear the hemiola: clap “OOM-pa-pa” twice and then “OOM……OOM……” in the same time.
How to tell it worked: at least three out of four groups compose and play their 4-bar tune; most explain that 7 has more ways and say how many tunes fit (1296); the class raises a hand when the hemiola sounds in the machine.

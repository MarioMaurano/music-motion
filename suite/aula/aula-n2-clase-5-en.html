What fits between two notes

Level 2 · Lesson 5 · the chromatic and the diatonic semitone, the diminished third and the comma · the 20th century with Bartók

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair says which of six steps from note to note are chromatic semitones (the note keeps its name: C → C♯) and which are diatonic (the note changes its name: E → F), and the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) sing the two semitones in the “Ave Maria” (D, D♭, C) and hear in Bach how a diminished third closes in on the note in the middle; (2) say which is higher on the tonal staircase, E♯ or F, and what that difference is called: the comma.

What you need: a computer with good speakers and internet, the Cosmic Keyboard, the board and, if possible, a projector. Three pieces in the Score, each with its own link: Schubert’s “Ave Maria”, Bach’s Sinfonia No. 9 and Bartók’s Román népi táncok (Romanian Folk Dances). It helps to have done Lesson 4.

Before the lesson (15 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Go through the nine slides of the monotono carousel in this guide (with the arrows, the dots or the ← → keys). Read the last one, “What fits in a tone”.
  • Open the “Ave Maria” and listen from second 42 (bar 10): the voice goes down D, D♭, C. Then, at 1:14 (bar 17), C, B, C. Sing both slowly.
  • Open Bach’s Sinfonia No. 9 and listen to the first 20 seconds (bars 1 to 6): the bass falls from A♭ to F♯ and rises to G; at the top, G♭ falls to E and rises to F. With the ♩ PULSE knob you can make it slower.
  • Open Bartók’s dances and find 1:25 (bar 65, “Pe loc”): the melody repeats the E♯. At 2:03 (bar 102) an F appears. If you prefer the Keyboard, the same dance is in recs › ◈ Tonal Mode.

Before we start: what fits in a tone

Two semitones that are not the same

In Lesson 4, the tone C–D was split with C♯ and with D♭. Now we look at the two pieces. There are two kinds of semitone, and the difference lies in the name of the notes:

  • Chromatic semitone: between two notes with the same name: C → C♯, D → D♭, E♭ → E. The note gets a sharp or a flat without moving on the stave.
  • Diatonic semitone: between two notes with different names: E → F, B → C, C♯ → D, D♭ → C. The note moves to a different line or space.

On the tonal staircase the two are not the same size: the chromatic one (2187/2048) is a little bigger than the diatonic one (256/243). One tone = one diatonic semitone + one chromatic semitone, in either order: D → E♭ → E, or D → D♯ → E. On the piano these words are not used: there the small step is simply the semitone, always the same, and C♯ and D♭ fall on the same key.

The words for each staircase

Each staircase has its own words, and it is best not to mix them. On the tempered one (the piano) two are enough: tone and semitone, because all the semitones are equal and a tone is two of them. On the piano, what is more, two names can give the same sound: this is enharmony. On the tonal one (the voice, the violin) there is no enharmony, and four words are needed: the tone, which is a little bigger than the piano’s; the chromatic semitone; the diatonic semitone; and the comma, which is the difference between the two semitones.

Schubert: the two semitones in one melody

In the “Ave Maria” (Lesson 2), the voice sings the two semitones one after the other. In bars 10 and 11 it goes down from D to D♭ (the same name: chromatic) and from D♭ to C (a different name: diatonic). In bars 17 and 19 it sings C, B, C: the piece is in B♭, and that B natural is the leading note, the note that pushes towards C. A singer tunes it a little higher than the piano so that it lands on the C: this is the tonal staircase from Lesson 4.

Bars 10 and 11 (second 42): D → D♭, chromatic semitone; D♭ → C, diatonic semitone. The notes, without the rhythm.

Bars 17 and 19 (1:14): the leading note, B, rises to C by a diatonic semitone.

Bach: the diminished third, tension and resolution

A diminished third encloses a single note between its two ends: A♭ and F♯ enclose G; G♭ and E enclose F. Each end is a diatonic semitone away from the note in the middle: A♭ tends to fall to G and F♯ to rise to G. On the piano, F♯–A♭ are the same keys as F♯–G♯, a tone. On the tonal staircase it is narrower than a tone (65536/59049 against 9/8), and that is why it sounds so tight: the ear is waiting for the enclosed note. This is the tension, and arriving on the note in the middle is its resolution.

Bach builds the theme of his Sinfonia No. 9 in F minor (BWV 795) out of this. In a single voice, he plays one end, leaps to the other and arrives on the enclosed note: in the bass, A♭ → F♯ → G and D♭ → B → C (bars 3 and 4); at the top, G♭ → E → F (bar 5); in the middle voice, the whole chain (bars 6 to 8). It comes back in bars 18 and 19 and in the ending, from bar 31. Mozart, Beethoven and almost every composer use this device to create tension and resolve it. The same Sinfonia is also in the Keyboard (recs › Built-in Demo · Bach): ◈ Tonal Mode and ⬦ Tempered, with the cyan strips; and in well-tempered tuning, in the Score (Werckmeister III).

The middle voice, bars 6 and 7: G♭ and E enclose F; D♭ and B enclose C. After the two ends comes the note in the middle. The notes, without the rhythm.

The time machine: the 20th century with Bartók

Hungary, 1906. Béla Bartók, pianist and composer, sets off with his friend Zoltán Kodály to travel round the villages with a phonograph, a machine that records on wax cylinders. They record country fiddlers, pipers and singers, and Bartók spends years writing those melodies down on paper. In 1915 he writes the Román népi táncok (Romanian Folk Dances) for piano, six dances from Transylvania. It is 20th-century music: Bartók does not want the country people to sound “as if in a drawing room”, but as they really are, with their turns of phrase, their rhythms and their notes.

Those musicians played by ear, on the tonal staircase, and Bartók wrote down what he heard. In the third dance, “Pe loc” (“in one place”), the melody repeats the E♯, and in the last two bars an F appears. In the sixth, “Mărunțel”, the right hand plays B♭ and A♯ within the same beat, two notes one after the other.

“Pe loc”: the E♯ (1:25, bar 65) and the F at the end (2:03, bar 102). In the recording, 1409.59 Hz and 1390.62 Hz: the E♯ is higher.

“Mărunțel”, bar 175 (3:31): B♭ at 463.54 Hz and A♯ at 469.86 Hz. On the piano, a single key.

The comma. The distance between E♯ and F, or between B♭ and A♯, is the same as between D♭ and C♯: the comma, the smallest interval in music. It is the amount by which the chromatic semitone is bigger than the diatonic one. It comes from a primary-school sum: twelve fifths, (3/2)¹², give 129.75; seven octaves, 2⁷, give 128. They do not match, and what is left over, 129.75 ÷ 128 = 1.0136, is the comma (531441/524288).

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Last lesson we saw that C♯ and D♭ are two different notes. Today we are going to look at what fits between two neighbouring notes, and we are going to hear it in Schubert, in Bach and in Bartók.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can tell a chromatic semitone from a diatonic one.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: C♯ and D♭

    With the Keyboard on 4–7, play 1 (C♯) and Shift+X (D♭). “Which one was higher?” The C♯. “And on the piano?” A single key. Write on the board: C — D♭ — C♯ — D.

  3. 17 min

    New material: two semitones and a tension

    1. The same name or a different name (6 min). On the board, under C — D♭ — C♯ — D, mark the steps yourself: C → C♯ “same name: chromatic”; C♯ → D “different name: diatonic”. Show the D → E slide of the carousel. Play D, D♯, E (X 2 C) and D, E♭, E (X Shift+C C): the two ways of splitting the tone. The class says “chromatic” or “diatonic” at each step.
    2. Schubert (5 min). Play the “Ave Maria” from second 42. Sing “D, D♭, C” yourself, softly; the class follows you. “Which step is chromatic and which is diatonic?” Then, from 1:14, “C, B, C”: the leading note that pushes towards C.
    3. Bach (6 min). Play the Sinfonia No. 9 from the beginning, for about 20 seconds. Write on the board A♭ → F♯ → G: “A♭ and F♯ enclose the G, and the bass arrives on it”. With both hands open, one above (A♭) and the other below (F♯), show the diminished third yourself; when the G sounds, the hands come together in the middle. The class makes the gesture with you while listening to the bass.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the carousel and the scores on the screen; without a screen, copy the D → E slide onto the board.

  4. 20 min

    Application: the carousel and Bartók

    The carousel (8 min). Project the slides one by one. On the first one, point out a chromatic and a diatonic semitone yourself. On the following ones, the whole class says “chromatic” or “diatonic” when you point; then one half of the class and then the other; finally pairs come to the front and do the pointing themselves. In each tone, someone shows where the comma is.

    Bartók (8 min). Tell the story of the phonograph. Play “Pe loc” from 1:25: “the melody repeats one note, the E♯”. At 2:03 the F appears. “Which one sounds higher to you?” The E♯, by a comma. If you play it on the Keyboard (◈ Tonal Mode), the class sees two different zones of the key light up.

    The sheet (4 min). Each pair marks the six steps on the sheet: chromatic or diatonic.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Read out the six steps on the sheet yourself; the pairs raise one finger for chromatic and two for diatonic, and the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

    “How do you recognise a chromatic semitone? Which note do A♭ and F♯ enclose? Which is higher, E♯ or F?”

    Listen to the answers: by the same name; G, the note in the middle; E♯, by a comma.

Challenge for the quick ones: “Each note can have a double flat, a flat, nothing, a sharp or a double sharp. How many sounds are there in an octave?” 7 × 5 = 35; the piano gathers them into 12 keys. And with a calculator, for the older ones: “Multiply 1.5 by itself twelve times (twelve fifths) and compare it with 2 multiplied by itself seven times (seven octaves).” 129.75 against 128: what is left over is the comma.
If they find it hard:
  • They confuse chromatic and diatonic: have them look only at the name of the note. Same name, chromatic; different name, diatonic. The ear comes later.
  • They can’t hear the comma: that is normal, it is very small. Have them look at the numbers and at the zones of the key: the eyes help the ears. In time, the ear learns it.
  • Bach’s Sinfonia goes too fast: slow it down with the ♩ PULSE knob and listen only to the bass.
  • The E♯ seems strange to them: it is. Many musicians think E♯ and F are the same; on the fiddles of the country people Bartók recorded, they are not.

What fits between two notes

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we learned that there are two kinds of semitone, the chromatic one (the note keeps its name, C → C♯) and the diatonic one (the note changes its name, E → F), and that between a neighbouring flat and sharp there is a very small distance: the comma. This week, play together:

  1. Same name or a different name? One person says two notes (“C, C sharp”, “E, F”) and the other answers “chromatic” or “diatonic”. Then swap.
  2. C, B, C: look up Schubert’s “Ave Maria” and sing that turn together, slowly. It is the note that “pushes” towards home.
  3. Hands that come together: listen to the beginning of Bach’s Sinfonia No. 9 (search for “Bach Sinfonia 9 BWV 795”). When the bass falls and rises, bring your hands together.
  4. Bartók: listen to the “Romanian Folk Dances”. Which one do you like best? Which one is for dancing in one place?
  5. The grandparents’ song: ask the grandparents about a song that people used to sing where they grew up, and record it on the phone, just as Bartók did with his phonograph.

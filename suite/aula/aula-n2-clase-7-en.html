In the classroom — The two clefs

The two clefs

Level 2 · Lesson 7 · the bass clef and the two-stave system · Antiquity with the kithara drone in the Song of Seikilos

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair reads out loud the nine notes of the drone in the Song of Seikilos, written in the bass clef, and one pair plays them on the Keyboard together with the melody; the class confirms with a thumbs-up.

Secondary objectives: (1) draw the bass clef and say which note lives on the fourth line; (2) explain which stave of a system carries the high notes and which the low ones.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, the Cosmic Keyboard, the Song of Seikilos in the Score (the same one as in Level 1, Lesson 4), the board and, if possible, a projector. It helps to have done Lesson 6.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Open the Song of Seikilos in the Score and listen to the whole piece (less than a minute). It now has two staves: on top, the melody of the aulos, in the treble clef; underneath, the drone of the kithara, in the bass clef, one long note per bar.
  • Read the drone with the picture in this guide: G, G, E, D, G, F, G, D, G.
  • On the Keyboard, the middle row plays that low octave: key G for G · F for F · D for E · S for D. Play the drone with the Song of Seikilos playing in the other tab: one key per bar, held long.

Before we start: the key to the low notes

The bass clef

The low notes would fall off the bottom of the stave in the treble clef. For them there is the bass clef: it begins with a fat dot on the fourth line, curls upwards and ends with two little dots on either side of that same line. That line is called F, the F below middle C, and the others are named from there.

The five lines, from bottom to top: G, B, D, F, A.

The four spaces: A, C, E, G.

The treble clef names the second line G; the bass clef, the fourth line F. Both work the same way: a sign that says which note lives on a line, and from there you count the steps (Lesson 6).

The system: two staves at once

When music has a high part and a low part, two staves are written one above the other and read at the same time, from left to right: this is a system. The top one carries the high notes and the bottom one the low notes. Piano music is always written like this: the right hand on top, in the treble clef, and the left hand underneath, in the bass clef, joined by a brace. Between the two staves, a ledger line in the middle is middle C: the same note, written from above or from below.

The time machine: the kithara in the Song of Seikilos

Greece, 1st century. In Level 1, Lesson 4, the class met the Seikilos stone and its melody, the oldest one that survives complete. The Greeks sang it or played it on the aulos, a double pipe, and accompanied it on the kithara, a large wooden lyre. The kithara held up the music with a drone: a long, low note in every bar, over which the melody moves.

In the Score, the Song of Seikilos now has both parts: the aulos on top, the kithara underneath. It is a system of two staves, and the bottom one is in the bass clef.

The beginning of the melody (aulos), in the treble clef.

The kithara drone, in the bass clef: one note per bar. G, G, E, D, G, F, G, D, G.

Did you know…? The C clef and the old clefs

There is a third clef, the C clef: its centre marks the line where middle C lives. On the third line it is the clef of the viola; on the fourth line, the clef for the high notes of the cello, the double bass, the bassoon and the trombone. That way each instrument writes its notes without leaving the stave.

C clef on the third line (the viola): middle C, on the middle line.

C clef on the fourth line (cello, double bass, bassoon, trombone): middle C, on the fourth line.

In old scores and choir scores other clefs appear: the treble clef on the first line, the bass clef on the third, the C clef on the first and on the second. They are the same idea on a different line. And when the notes go too high or too low, ledger lines are used, or a dotted line with 8va (“an octave higher”) or 8vb (“an octave lower”): that way you don’t have to write so many little lines.

Canelo learns the bass clef

The bass clef is a step where many children get stuck. In the workbook, Canelo plays the pupil: he asks, gets it wrong and works it out with them. You can use his sentences in class:

  • “So where do the low notes live? They fall off the stave!” (Dad’s voice, the big drum, the double bass).
  • “The two little dots hug a line: that line is F!”
  • “From F I go up and down, step by step, just as in the treble clef.”
  • His trick: first look at which line the clef hangs on. If it curls round the 2nd line, that line is G; if it hangs on the 4th, that line is F. “That’s so easy!”
  • His mistake: he reads a note on the second line as if it were in the treble clef and says “G”, but in the bass clef it is B. “First I look at the clef, and only then do I read the note!”

Listening to what sounds underneath. In the Score, the class watches the bass-clef stave while the music plays: the kithara drone in the Song of Seikilos, the left hand of “Alla Turca” and the accompaniment of the “Ave Maria”. “What is the bottom part doing while the melody sings?”

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Do you remember the Seikilos stone? Today we are going to read the part the lyre played, in a new clef, and we are going to play it together with the melody.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can read the notes in the bass clef.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the treble clef

    Draw a treble clef yourself and ask: “Which note lives on the second line?” G. The class names two or three more notes in the treble clef with you, counting from G. Retell the story of the Seikilos stone in two sentences.

  3. 17 min

    New material: the bass clef and the system

    1. Drawing the clef (5 min). Draw the bass clef yourself, slowly: the fat dot on the fourth line, the curl and the two little dots. The class draws it in the air and then on their sheet. “Which line do the two dots go round?” The fourth: F.
    2. Reading from F (6 min). From the F on the fourth line, count upwards and downwards yourself: A on the fifth, D on the third. Write the lines (G, B, D, F, A) and the spaces (A, C, E, G). Point to notes; first the whole class says the name, then one half, then the other.
    3. The system (6 min). Project the Song of Seikilos in the Score with ⛶ FULL. “Which is the stave for the high notes? And the one for the low notes?” Press ▶: the class follows the long notes at the bottom with a finger in the air.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the score on the screen; without a screen, copy the drone onto the board.

  4. 20 min

    Application: playing the kithara

    Reading the drone (6 min). On the board or on the screen, the class reads out loud the nine notes of the drone: G, G, E, D, G, F, G, D, G. Find each one together on the keyboard: G, G, D, S, G, F, G, S, G.

    Playing with the Song of Seikilos (10 min). First play the drone yourself on the Keyboard while the Song of Seikilos in the Score is playing: one key per bar, held long. Then pairs take turns: one points to the note on the stave and the other plays it; the class sings the melody softly or listens.

    The sheet (4 min). Each pair names the six bass-clef notes on the sheet.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. The class reads the drone out loud together one last time, while you point.

    “Which note lives on the fourth line of the bass clef? Which stave of a system carries the low notes? What did the kithara play?”

    Listen to the answers: F; the bottom one; the drone, a long note in every bar.

Challenge for the quick ones: “The drone uses four notes: G, F, E and D. What interval is there between the lowest and the highest?” D–G: D, E, F, G, a perfect fourth. And one more: “Middle C is written on the ledger line below the stave in the treble clef. Where is it written in the bass clef?” On the ledger line above.
If they find it hard:
  • They read the bass clef as if it were the treble clef: it is the most common mistake. Have them colour in the fourth line, F, and always count from it.
  • They get lost in the system: have them put a ruler under the bottom stave and follow only that one.
  • The drone and the melody don’t match up: slow the Song of Seikilos down with the ♩ PULSE knob, and have the pair play the note when each bar begins, with the first note of the melody.

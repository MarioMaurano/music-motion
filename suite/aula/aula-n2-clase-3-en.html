In the classroom — Fast notes and ornaments

Fast notes and ornaments

Level 2 · Lesson 3 · demisemiquavers and hemidemisemiquavers, the triplet, the trill and the mordent, written in the Score · the Baroque with Bach and Sinfonia No. 9 in three tunings

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class and then in groups of 4 or 5. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each group writes a bar of 2/4 with demisemiquavers or with a triplet, and an ornament; they play it or clap it, and the class says which note values and which ornament they used.

Secondary objectives: (1) say how many demisemiquavers fit in a crotchet (8) and in a quaver (4); (2) name the three tunings in which Bach’s Sinfonia No. 9 is heard.

What you need: a computer with speakers and a projector, the Cosmic Keyboard, the Score (with the school account, ideally one computer per group), Sinfonia No. 9 in the Score and “Alla Turca”. It helps to have done Lesson 2 (the dot and semiquavers).

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Listen to the beginning of Sinfonia No. 9 in the Score and find the mordent sign in bar 12.
  • In the Score, write a bar with the NUM PAD: 5 crotchet, 4 quaver, 3 semiquaver, 2 demisemiquaver, 1 hemidemisemiquaver. Play it back.
  • Try the triplet: select a note and open ³ TUPLET in its menu. And an ornament: the trill and the mordent are among the articulations (ART).

Before we start: faster than the semiquaver

The stave is not a keyboard

Each line and each space of the stave is a step of the scale (C, D, E, F…), not a key on the piano. The stave tells you which step is played, but not how big each step is: that is decided by the key and by the musician’s ear. A ♯ or a ♭ changes the name of the note on that line or in that space; it does not mean “the black key”.

C and D are on neighbouring steps; F♯ lives in the space of F, with its sharp. Nowhere does it say which key to play.

All semitones being the same size is an agreement made for the piano: the tempered system. The voice and the violin build the staircase in a different way, and the same written note can sound slightly different depending on who plays it. You will see and hear this in Lesson 4.

The chocolate bar keeps splitting

In Level 1 the semibreve was a whole bar of chocolate, which was split into minims, crotchets and quavers. In Lesson 2 came the semiquaver, half of the quaver. The chocolate bar keeps splitting: half of the semiquaver is the demisemiquaver (1/32 of the semibreve) and half of the demisemiquaver, the hemidemisemiquaver (1/64). Each time the note lasts half as long, it gets one more beam.

quavers
1/8 · 1 beam
semiquavers
1/16 · 2 beams
demisemiquavers
1/32 · 3 beams
hemidemisemiquavers
1/64 · 4 beams

A crotchet holds 2 quavers, 4 semiquavers, 8 demisemiquavers or 16 hemidemisemiquavers. These are the notes of fast passages and of ornaments.

The triplet: three instead of two

Sometimes we want three equal notes in the time of two. Three quavers are written with a 3 above them: that is a triplet. Together the three last as long as a crotchet, just like two ordinary quavers. We say “tri-pe-ta” on one beat, instead of “ti-ti”.

Two quavers on one beat: “ti-ti”.

A triplet on one beat: “tri-pe-ta”.

Ornaments

Ornaments are fast notes that decorate a main note. They are not written out note by note: a small sign above the note says what to do.

trill
the note and its upper neighbour, alternating quickly
mordent
the note, its lower neighbour and the note again
upper mordent
with the upper neighbour

The appoggiatura is a small note before the main one, which leans on it; if it has a little line through it, it is an acciaccatura and is played as fast as possible. “Alla Turca” from Lesson 1 is full of them.

The time machine: Bach, the Baroque and Sinfonia No. 9

Germany, 1720. Johann Sebastian Bach writes a book for his nine-year-old son Wilhelm Friedemann, to learn to play the keyboard. On one of its first pages he draws him a table of ornaments, with each sign and the notes to play. In the Baroque, ornaments were part of the style: the musician decorated the melody, and anyone who could do it well was admired.

Three years later Bach makes a fair copy of his 15 Sinfonias, pieces in three voices for his pupils. No. 9, in F minor, is one of the most intense. It can be heard in three tunings:

  • Tonal (Pythagorean), in the Score, or in the Keyboard: recs › Built-in Demo · Bach › ◈ Tonal Mode.
  • Well-tempered (Werckmeister III), the tuning of many keyboards in Bach’s time, in the Score.
  • Equal temperament, the tuning of today’s piano, in the Keyboard: recs › Built-in Demo · Bach › ⬦ Tempered.

All 15 Sinfonias, in the three versions, are in the demos zip file. Lesson 4 explains why the three sound different.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Some music runs so fast that the semiquaver is not enough, and some musicians decorate every note. Today we are going to write those fast notes and those ornaments.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can write fast notes, a triplet and an ornament.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the chocolate bar and the semiquaver

    Draw a crotchet on the board. “How many quavers fit in it? And semiquavers?” Two and four. Clap together one beat with two quavers (“ti-ti”) and one with four semiquavers (“ti-ki-ti-ki”).

  3. 17 min

    New material: demisemiquavers, the triplet and ornaments

    1. The demisemiquaver and the hemidemisemiquaver (5 min). Keep splitting the chocolate bar on the board: the demisemiquaver, with three beams; the hemidemisemiquaver, with four. “How many demisemiquavers fit in a crotchet?” Eight. Clap eight very fast claps in one beat.
    2. The triplet (4 min). Clap “ti-ti” and then “tri-pe-ta”, on the same beat. The class follows you, alternating.
    3. Ornaments (4 min). Sing a long note, then the same note with a trill; then with a mordent. Listen to bar 12 of Sinfonia No. 9 and to the appoggiaturas in “Alla Turca”.
    4. Bach in three tunings (4 min). Play the beginning of Sinfonia No. 9 in tonal and in equal temperament. “Do they sound the same?” Note down what they say; the answer comes in Lesson 4.

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the Score on the screen with ⛶ FULL; without a screen, draw the note values on the board.

  4. 20 min

    Application: a bar with fast notes and an ornament

    On paper (7 min). Write an example on the board yourself: a bar of 2/4 with a triplet, a crotchet and a trill. Each group writes its own: two beats, with demisemiquavers or a triplet, and an ornament on one note.

    In the Score (10 min). Groups with a computer write it in the Score with the NUM PAD (5 crotchet, 4 quaver, 3 semiquaver, 2 demisemiquaver, 1 hemidemisemiquaver), the triplet with ³ TUPLET and the ornament from ART. They play it back and correct it. Without a computer, they clap it and sing it.

    Guess (3 min). Each group plays or claps its bar without showing it; the class says which note values and which ornament they used.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board.

    “How many demisemiquavers fit in a crotchet? And in a quaver? What is a triplet? In which three tunings did we hear Bach?”

    Listen to the answers: eight; four; three notes in the time of two; tonal, well-tempered and equal temperament.

A challenge for the quick ones: “How many hemidemisemiquavers fit in a semibreve?” Sixty-four: that is why it is 1/64. And one more: “In 2/4, how many quaver triplets fit in a bar?” Two, one per beat.
If they find it hard:
  • They get lost with the beams: have them count the beams: one, two, three, four; each beam splits the note in half.
  • Their triplet comes out as two quavers plus one more: have them say “tri-pe-ta” with the clap only on “tri”.
  • They cannot find the ornament in the Score: first they select the note and then open ART.

After this lesson: a review with Don Quixote

The pupil’s workbook closes chapters 1 to 3 with a review: “Don Quixote sets off on a journey”. In 1605 Miguel de Cervantes published the novel; more than a hundred years later, Georg Philipp Telemann turned it into music in his suite Burlesque de Quixotte. It is a good opportunity to show how literature and music have gone hand in hand for centuries.

Listen to “Le Galope de Rosinante” and, straight after it, Sancho Panza’s donkey. Each bar has three quavers. Rocinante gallops with four semiquavers and a quaver; the donkey plods along with a dotted quaver and three semiquavers. Clap them together and then make up a dance: the knights leap to the gallop, the squires walk with the donkey, and when the music changes, the steps change.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four groups write a bar of 2/4 that adds up correctly, with demisemiquavers or a triplet and an ornament; the class guesses the note values of most groups; and most pupils can say how many demisemiquavers fit in a crotchet and name the three tunings of Sinfonia No. 9.

Notes that run and notes that get decorated

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today we met the fastest notes (demisemiquavers and hemidemisemiquavers), the triplet and ornaments, and we listened to Bach in three tunings. This week, play together:

  1. The chocolate bar: with a bar of chocolate (or a sheet of paper), split it into halves, quarters, eighths… how far can you go? Your child tells you the name of each note value.
  2. Bach twice: listen to Sinfonia No. 9 in the Score and then on the keyboard (musicmotionsuite.com/apps/mmk-demo.html, recs › Built-in Demo · Bach), in Tempered. Can you hear the difference?
  3. The trill: ask your child to play you a trill on the keyboard, alternating two neighbouring keys very fast.
  4. Tri-pe-ta: say “tri-pe-ta” and “ti-ti” together, one beat each, clapping the beat. Which one fits in the same clap? Both!

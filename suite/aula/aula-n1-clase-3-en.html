In the classroom — The house of the notes

The house of the notes

Level 1 · Lesson 3 · the stave

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 8 to 12, the whole class. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair finds on the keyboard a note that the teacher points to on the stave on the board, counting the steps from the first note; the class gives a thumbs-up if it is the right one.

Secondary objectives: (1) count the lines and spaces of the stave from the bottom up; (2) show with the body whether a note is on a line or in a space.

What you need: a computer with speakers, the board and, if available, a projector. A sheet with five lines for each pupil, or a music manuscript book. It helps to have done Lesson 2. No music knowledge needed: the keyboard does the work; you guide.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise the keyboard. In class your attention needs to be on the children, not on the buttons. Repeat these steps until you can do them without looking at the guide:

  • Open the Cosmic Keyboard. Play Z X C V B N M A slowly: this is the eight-note staircase you are going to draw on the stave. The first seven are on the bottom row; A, on the middle row, is the eighth step.
  • Practise the hidden note: play a single key, for example B, and count from Z until you reach it: “Z one, X two, C three, V four, B five”. It is the fifth note of the staircase.
  • Before the pupils arrive, draw five long lines on the board, about a finger and a half apart. Have the five-line sheets ready.

Before we start: the house of the notes

The stave: a five-storey house

The stave is five identical lines on which music is written. The lines are counted from the bottom up: line 1 is the bottom one and line 5 the top one. Between the lines there are four spaces, which are also counted from the bottom.

On a line or in a space

A note can live on a line (the line goes through its middle) or in a space (it sits between two lines, without either of them touching it). The higher the note, the higher it sounds: it is the “drawing of the sound” from Lesson 2, now with fixed floors so that everyone draws it the same way.

One note on each step: line, space, line, space… The black notes live on a line; the coloured ones, in a space.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Today we are going to get to know the house where the notes live. At the end, every pair will play on the keyboard the note I point to in the house.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can find a note on the stave and play it.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the drawing of the sound

    Play three or four sounds, some from the bottom row and some from the top row. The class plays the lift game from Lesson 2: crouching for low, standing for high. Then a pupil draws on the board what they heard, with dashes up or down.

    “How do we know how high each dash should go? What if the house of sound had floors, so that we all draw the same way?”

  3. 17 min

    New material: the five-storey house

    1. Counting the floors (3 min). Show the stave on the board. Count first yourself, pointing to each line from the bottom up: “one, two, three, four, five”. The class counts with you a second time. Then the spaces: “one, two, three, four”. Game: you point to a line or a space and the class says its number; first the whole class, then one half and then the other.
    2. Line or space? (6 min). Draw a note (a circle) on a line and show the gesture yourself: arms crossed, like a line going through it. Draw another in a space: hands pressed together, like something squeezed between two walls. Now draw notes in different places; the whole class answers, then one half and the other, and finally the pairs, facing each other.
    3. The staircase in the house (5 min). Draw eight notes going up: line, space, line, space… as in the picture in “Before we start”. Play Z X C V B N M A slowly while you point to each note; the class sings on an “ah”. Repeat with a volunteer doing the pointing. Then from the top down. Each key is one step of the house: from a line to the next space, or from a space to the next line.

    “Does this note live on the top floor or the bottom floor? Will it sound high or low?”

    With an interactive whiteboard or a projector, the class sees the picture on the screen; without a screen, copy it on to the board. (In the next lesson they will see that, with the treble clef, the first C lives on a little line of its own, a ledger line, below the house. For now, what matters is that each key goes up one step.)

  4. The key to C (3 min). Tell the class: “About a thousand years ago, monks wrote music on four lines. To know which note was which, they put a letter at the start of a line: C. That letter was the first clef (the word “clef” means “key”).” Write a C at the start of one line of the stave on the board: the note on that line is C. The class sings it and climbs the staircase from there: C, D, E… Then rub out the C, put it on another line and repeat: the same house, with a different key. In the pupil’s workbook, My Little Music Grammar · Level 1 (chapter 3), each pupil chooses their line and colours in the notes with the colours of Canelo’s mat.
  5. 20 min

    Application: the hidden note

    On the sheet (8 min). Each pupil draws the eight-note staircase on their sheet, just like the one on the board. You play a single key of the staircase. The first time, everyone counts aloud together, with a finger on the sheet, from the first note: “one, two, three…” while you play again from Z until you reach the hidden key. After that, each pupil looks for it alone and shows it with a finger to their partner.

    Playing the note you point to (12 min). Now the other way round. Show it yourself first: point to a note on the board, for example the one in space 2 (the fourth), and count the steps aloud from the bottom: “one, two, three, four”. Then count the keys from Z: “Z, X, C, V” and play V.

    1. A pair comes up to the computer. You point to a note of the staircase on the board.
    2. One of the pair counts the steps on the board; the other counts the keys from Z and plays the note.
    3. The class gives a thumbs-up if it is the right one. To check, play the staircase yourself from Z up to that note while the class counts.
    4. The pairs take turns and swap roles. While one pair plays, the rest point to the note on their sheet.
  6. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Point to one last note; the pairs who have not had a turn yet play it, and the class gives a thumbs-up if it is the right one.

    “How did you find the note on the keyboard? Where did you start counting?”

    Listen to the answers: counting from the bottom, line and space one at a time, and always starting on Z.

With the Melody Machine: the house of the notes

To end the lesson, or to start the next one. Open the Melody Machine and press SPIN.

  1. Line or space? Point to a note in the first bar: the class says whether it is on a line or in a space, and on which floor of the house.
  2. The shining note. Press READ: a note lights up. A volunteer counts the steps from the previous note and says whether it went up or down.
  3. Listening to the house. Press PLAY and follow the notes with your finger on the screen: when the melody goes up, the finger goes up a floor.
If they find it hard:
  • They count from the top: draw an arrow pointing up next to the stave and write “1” beside the bottom line. Repeat together: “we count the house from the ground up”.
  • They mix up line and space: draw the note big and ask: “Does the line go through its middle, or is the note between two lines?” Ask them to trace it with a finger on their sheet.
  • They get lost counting the steps: count aloud with a finger on each note, and sing “C, D, E…” as you go up. If needed, draw only four notes and add the others once they can find them.
  • They play a different key: play together from Z, key by key, while a classmate points to each step on the board, until you reach the note.

The staircase for this lesson: from Z to M on the bottom row, and A on the middle row, which is the next C, the eighth step.

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note.

How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs play on the keyboard the note pointed to on the stave, and the class confirms it with a thumbs-up. In addition, most pupils count the lines from the bottom and show with their body whether a note is on a line or in a space.

These lessons are a starting point. Each teacher can design the examples to suit their own class. For our part, we will keep adding lesson topics according to the requests we receive through the contact form.

The house of the notes, at home

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class we learned that music is written on five lines, the stave, and that the notes live on the lines or in the spaces. This week, play together:

  1. Open the keyboard on the computer: musicmotionsuite.com/apps/mmk-demo.html
  2. The house on the floor: lay five pieces of string or strips of tape on the floor, one step apart. One person says “line 2” or “space 3”; the other jumps there. Then swap. Remember: line 1 is the one closest to you.
  3. Line or space? On a sheet with five lines, one person draws a note; the other says where it lives.
  4. The staircase: the child draws eight notes going up (line, space, line…) and plays them on the keyboard from Z to M and then A, while the adult points to each note.
  5. The Melody Machine: open musicmotionsuite.com/suite/tragamonedas.html, press SPIN and look at the notes: which are on a line and which are in a space? With PRINT you can print the melody and colour in the notes on the lines. No registration needed.

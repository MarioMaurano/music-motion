In the classroom — Our melody

Our melody

Level 1 · Lesson 9 · from the machine to our own melody: composing, adding words and performing it

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 8 to 12, in groups of 4 pupils (if there are pupils left over, a group of 5 has two pupils write one bar together). Length: 50 minutes, plus one session for the concert. This is the last lesson of Level 1.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each group has its own 8-bar melody that begins and ends on C: bars 1 to 4 were chosen in the Melody Machine and bars 5 to 8 were written by the members of the group, one each. The group adds words to it, and sings and plays the whole melody in time with the beat.

Secondary objectives: (1) write a bar of 4/4 with notes from C to G whose note values add up to 4; (2) put one syllable on each crotchet and two on each pair of quavers; (3) explain why one ending sounds “at home” and another “up in the air”.

What you need: a computer with speakers (ideally one per group), the board and, if available, a projector; the Melody Machine and, if available, a printer. The bar strips to cut out (at the end of this guide), scissors and pencils; the note-value cards from Lesson 8 help. It helps to have done the whole of Level 1. No music knowledge needed: the rule is simple and the class’s ears decide.

Before the lesson (15 minutes, at home): prepare the strips and practise.

  • Print the strips (five per sheet) and cut them out, one per pupil.
  • Open the Melody Machine, press SPIN several times, keep a bar you like with HOLD and press PRINT: out comes a sheet with bars 1 to 4 and four empty bars for 5 to 8. If there is no printer in the classroom, print one sheet per group beforehand, each with a different melody.
  • Write the example bars A, B, C and D from this guide on four strips. Open the Cosmic Keyboard and the metronome (pulse, about 60 BPM with tap, start and click, as in Lesson 1), and play them in two orders: A B C D (it ends on C and sounds “at home”) and A D C B (it ends on G, up high: it “stays up in the air”).
  • On the bottom bar of the Keyboard, the ● wav button records whatever is playing: try it out, you will use it at the concert.
  • Have the demos of “Are You Sleeping, Brother John?” and “Ode to Joy” ready for the challenge, with the ♩ PULSE knob for the tempo.

Before we start: a melody is a journey

Leaving home and coming back

In this lesson the pupils stop reading other people’s music and write their own. First the Melody Machine gives them four bars that already fit together: that way composing begins as a game and stops being frightening. Then each pupil writes one of the missing bars, from 5 to 8, using everything they have learned in this level: the notes from C to G on the treble clef (Lesson 4), note values and rests (Lessons 5 and 6) and adding up to 4 (Lessons 7 and 8). At the end, all together, they add words.

A melody is like a journey: it leaves home, wanders about and comes back. In C major, home is C. If the melody ends on C, and on a long note, the ear feels it has arrived; if it ends on another note, it feels it “stays up in the air”. It is not a rule to obey, it is something you can hear: that is why the class votes by listening.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “Today each group is going to make its own song. The machine gives us the first four bars, we write the other four and add the words. Then we are going to perform it in a concert.”

    Write the goal on the board: We can compose our song and sing it together.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: reading a bar

    Stick strip A on the board. Read it yourself first: the note names (“C, D, E, C”), the rhythm syllables (“ta, ta, ta, ta”) and the sum (1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4). Then the class reads strip B in the same way and a volunteer plays it.

  3. 12 min

    New material: the journey and the machine

    1. A four-bar journey (4 min). Stick the four strips up in the order A B C D and play them one after the other with the metronome. Then in the order A D C B. Ask: “Which one ends at home? Which one stays up in the air?” The class votes with a thumbs-up and explains why.
    2. The machine chooses the first four (8 min). Open the Melody Machine in front of the class: SPIN and PLAY. “Does it end at home?” Yes: the fourth bar always comes back to C. Taking turns, each group spins until it has four bars it likes; with HOLD it keeps the bar it wants to save. When the group is happy, press PRINT (or give them the sheet you printed beforehand).
  4. 25 min

    Application: our bars and our words

    1. Bars 5 to 8 (10 min). Write a strip yourself in front of the class, thinking out loud: “I start on E, I go up to G with a minim… how much have I got? 1 + 1 + 2 = 4, done.” Each pupil in the group writes one bar on their strip, following the composer’s rules on the pupil sheet; whoever writes bar 8 ends on a long C. The group plays them one after the other on the computer with the metronome and tries other orders; if they cannot agree, let a dice decide, as in the 1792 game. Then they copy the four bars onto the sheet, in the empty bars. That makes four bars, two for each line of the verse: the first line in bars 5 and 6, the second in bars 7 and 8. In the workbook My Little Music Grammar · Level 1 (chapter 9) each pupil practises four bars on two staves, one line on each, and “The group sheet” (page 27) collects the complete song: bars 1 to 4 from the machine, bars 5 to 8 from the group, the words, and who sings, who plays and who introduces the song.
    2. Adding words (10 min). Show it yourself first on strip A, four crotchets: one syllable on each note, “Off-we-go-now”. The rule: one syllable on each crotchet, two on each pair of quavers (“ti-ti”) and, on the long note, the vowel is stretched (“ho-o-o-ome”). They write the words at the top, in the free space on the sheet, and under each note its syllable. The group chooses the topic: something they like, something that worries them or something that makes them laugh (break time, homework, where they live, food, their family). It can be serious or funny.
    3. Singing and playing (5 min). Each group sings its song once while one member plays it on the keyboard, or the machine plays it with PLAY. The others listen: “Does it end at home?”

    “We can laugh at things and at situations, and at ourselves too, but never at a classmate or at anyone for the way they are, the way they speak or where they come from.”

    Say this before they start writing the words: good teasing is clever, not hurtful.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal and Level 1

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Ask:

    “What made one song sound better than another? What have you learned in this level that you didn’t know before?”

    Look back with the class over the journey: the singing staircase, high and low, the home note, the treble clef, the chocolate bar, the ti-ti and the rest, the bar and its numbers. Each pupil says which part they liked best.

A challenge for the quick ones: “In how many different ways can the group’s 4 bars be put in order?” For the first place there are 4 possible strips, for the second 3… 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. “And if there were 8 bars?” Answer: 8 × 7 × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 40,320. That is why there is no need to try them all: the ear chooses. (The oldest song in the world, which they will meet in Level 2, also has 8 bars.) And groups who finish early can read and play “Are You Sleeping, Brother John?”, which is sung as a round, or Beethoven’s “Ode to Joy”, which has a dotted crotchet: one piece plus half a piece more.

The concert: the voice of the class

In another session, or as an end-of-level celebration with the families invited. Each group performs its song: before singing, they say in one sentence what it is about. Anyone who wants to can sing a verse on their own. Record each song with ● wav on the Keyboard: it is the keepsake of Level 1.

Rehearsals. Before the concert, each group rehearses its song several times: slowly first and then at its own tempo. Decide who starts, how everyone comes in and how they all finish together, and who introduces the song. You handle the recording from the laptop; if anyone plays an instrument, they can bring it and join in.

The group’s score in the Score. Before the concert, prepare each group’s song: in the Melody Machine, with the four bars the group chose, press SAVE .mmf and open it in the Score. There, add bars 5 to 8 that the group wrote and the words under each note. At the concert you project it: the class and the families see the score while it plays, and the group sings reading its own music.

Costumes and props. There is no need to buy anything: each group chooses one detail that goes with its song. For the verses, something from the rhyme or from where you live: a hat, a shawl, a neckerchief, a colourful skirt. For a rock song, some dark glasses or a jacket; for a song from another time, a detail from that time, such as a bow tie or a paper wig like Mozart’s. And some props that make a sound: shakers made from a bottle and rice, two sticks to mark the beat, scarves for dancing.

All the arts in one concert. The concert brings together the arts the course has touched on: music (the melody they composed), poetry (the verses and Edward Lear), dance (the dance of the bars from Lesson 7), drama (introducing themselves, coming on and going off together, looking at the audience) and art (a poster with the group’s name or the decorations for the playground). Music does not replace the other arts: it brings them together.

The teacher chooses with the class how to present the songs:

  1. The group’s song, just as they composed it, with its words.
  2. Verses. The melody repeats and each group, or each pupil, sings a new verse about their world: what they like, what worries them, what makes them laugh. It can be funny, but always respectful.
  3. A song from another time, in their own way. A well-known melody, such as “Ode to Joy”, with their own words and style.

Local musical forms are welcome: the nursery rhyme, rap, cumbia, the playground song or whatever is sung where you live. Let them use their own words and expressions: music also tells where we come from.

An example of verses: “Hey Diddle Diddle”

“Hey Diddle Diddle” is a very old English nursery rhyme (first printed around 1765) in which every line is a new, impossible scene: the cat plays the fiddle, the cow jumps over the moon, the little dog laughs and the dish runs away with the spoon. Many children know it from home or from school:

Hey diddle diddle,
The cat and the fiddle,
The cow jumped over the moon;
The little dog laughed
To see such sport,
And the dish ran away with the spoon.

In the same spirit, here are two verses: a question and its answer. Each line has eight syllables, and the strong syllable (in capitals) is always the seventh. The lines end with a rhyme, but in a verse like this the rhyme does not need to be strict: it is enough for the second and fourth lines to rhyme (dancing / prancing, spoon now / moon now), and sometimes only the vowels match. The poet Edward Lear (1812–1888) also played with the music of words: in “The Owl and the Pussy-cat” (1871) the owl plays a small guitar and sings by the light of the moon. It begins: “The Owl and the Pussy-cat went to sea / In a beautiful pea-green boat”.

Hey there, Cat, with your old FIDdle,
why is everybody DANCing?
Why’s the cow up in the STARlight,
and the little dog out PRANCing?

I’ve a party in the MEAdow,
and the dish is with the SPOON now;
if you hear the music PLAYing,
come and dance up to the MOON now!

The example melody. Each line takes up two bars: six syllables in the first (ti-ti ti-ti ta ta) and the strong syllable on the minim of the second, on the first beat. Line 2 ends on G and sounds like a question; line 4 ends on C, the ending. The answer verse is sung to the same melody.

Listen to the two verses in the Score, with the words under each note.

Counting with claps. “Hey-there-Cat-with-your-old-fid-dle”: eight claps. When a line ends on a strong syllable, like “The cow jumped over the MOON”, it has one syllable fewer and the last note is held longer.

Or with their own melody. Each group can get its own from the Melody Machine: SPIN, 8 bars in 4/4. To make the verse fit, the strong syllable of each line goes on the first beat of every second bar. If there is a note too many, repeat the syllable; if there is one too few, split a note in two. That way the line and the bar adjust to each other.

“In the rhyme, the little dog laughed to see such sport. Who will write the little dog’s verse?”

Did you know…? More than two hundred years ago, composers were already playing this game. In 1792 a “musical dice game” was published under Mozart’s name: for each bar of a minuet there were eleven written versions, and two dice chose which one was played. As they all fitted together, every throw gave a different minuet, and there are so many that nobody could play them all in a lifetime. The Melody Machine is a modern dice game: the reels suggest, and then the ear decides.
If they find it hard:
  • They don’t know what to write: let them start with a repeated note and change only the rhythm, or take a piece of the staircase (C D E F).
  • The sum doesn’t make 4: lay the cards from Lesson 8 on the strip; each beat is one box.
  • The relay breaks down: the metronome does not stop; whoever finishes counts out loud “3, 4” and the next one comes in on “one”.
  • They can’t find words: let them start with their own name, a food or a pet, and count the syllables with claps, one per beat.
  • A joke upsets someone: change the word together; remind them of the sentence about respect. Laughing together, yes; laughing at someone, no.
  • The group can’t agree: try both orders and vote by listening; if it is a tie, the composer of the last bar decides.

To compose, the bottom row is enough, from Z (C) to B (G).

Note names: C = Do · D = Re · E = Mi · F = Fa · G = Sol · A = La · B = Si. Some materials use the syllables: both name the same note.

How to tell it worked: each group has its sheet with an 8-bar melody that begins and ends on C, with words, and sings it all the way through in time with the beat. In addition, most of the bars written add up to 4, the syllables fall on the notes, and the pupils explain in their own words why their song ends “at home”.

These lessons are a starting point. Each teacher can design the examples to suit their own class. For our part, we will keep adding lesson topics according to the requests we receive through the contact form.

Our melody, at home

For parents · 10 minutes a week · no music knowledge needed

Today in class each group made its own song: the Melody Machine gave them the first four bars, they wrote the other four and added words. This week, play together:

  1. Open the keyboard on the computer: musicmotionsuite.com/apps/mmk-demo.html
  2. A family bar: on a strip of paper with five lines, your child writes a bar with notes from C to G that add up to 4 beats; then they play it on the keyboard (C is the Z key).
  3. Adding words: look for words that fit the rhythm of the bar (the name of someone at home, a food, a pet) and sing it.
  4. Two bars, two orders: if each of you writes a bar, play them in one order and then the other. Which one ends “at home”?
  5. The Melody Machine: open musicmotionsuite.com/suite/tragamonedas.html, press SPIN and PLAY. The machine does the same as the class: four bars that fit together. With HOLD you keep the bar you like and carry on spinning until you have your favourite melody. With PRINT you print it and write bars 5 to 8 together. No registration needed.

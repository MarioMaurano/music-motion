In the classroom — Chord figures and the piano

Chord figures and the piano

Level 2 · Lesson 10 · Part 2 · inversions, chord figures, the seventh chord on V · the piano: the left hand that hardly moves

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class and then in groups. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, the class reads the chord figures of a piano piece from the harmony machine (I, IV⁶₄, V⁶₅) and says, for each chord, which note is at the bottom.

Secondary objectives: (1) play the path I – IV⁶₄ – V⁶₅ – I on the Keyboard; (2) write on a sheet the Roman numeral heard in each bar.

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, the Cosmic Keyboard, the harmony machine, one sheet printed with BLANK per pupil (how to do it is explained below) and, if possible, a projector. This lesson follows Part 1.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • On the Keyboard, play A D G (C major), A F H (F major with C at the bottom) and M F G (B, F and G), and go back to A D G. Notice that your hand hardly moves.
  • In the harmony machine, press PIANO, CLASSICAL, LEVEL 2 · CHORDS, 4 bars, and then SPIN and PLAY. Look at the chord figures under the bass clef.
  • Print the sheets: with the same settings press BLANK and, in the Score, PAGE and PRINT.

Before we start: which note is at the bottom

Inversions

The three notes of a chord can be arranged in three ways, and the chord is still the same. When the root is at the bottom, the chord is in root position. When the bottom note is the third, it is in first inversion; when it is the fifth, in second inversion.

C major: C-E-G, E-G-C and G-C-E. The same three notes.

On the Keyboard: Z C B (root position), C B A (first inversion) and B A D (second inversion).

Chord figures: the small number

The Roman numeral tells you the degree, the step of the key. The small number next to it tells you which note of the chord is at the bottom. It counts the intervals upwards from the lowest note.

  • No number (I, IV, V): the root is at the bottom. This is root position.
  • 6 (I6): the third is at the bottom; the root is a sixth higher. This is first inversion.
  • ⁶₄ (I⁶₄): the fifth is at the bottom; above it there is a fourth and a sixth. This is second inversion.

In C major: C-E-G is I; E-G-C is I6; G-C-E is I⁶₄. And C-F-A is the chord of F (the IV) with its fifth, C, at the bottom: IV⁶₄.

The seventh chord on V

A fourth note can be added to the chord on V, still taking one and skipping one: in C major, G-B-D and, on top, F. This is V7, the dominant seventh chord. The new note gives it more tension: it sounds like a question that wants to go back to I.

With four notes the figures change a little. V⁶₅ is V7 with its third, B, at the bottom. On the piano it is often played with three notes: B, F and G (the D is left out and the ear fills it in).

Why the piano uses inversions

If the left hand played every chord in root position, it would jump from one place to another. With inversions, the fingers stay where they are and only one or two notes change: I – IV⁶₄ – V⁶₅ – I is the most comfortable path, the one that appears in so many pieces for beginners.

On the Keyboard: A D G (I) · A F H (IV⁶₄) · M F G (V⁶₅) · A D G (I). The low C stays still in the first two, and the high G in the last two.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “The harmony machine writes numbers under the piano music. Today we are going to learn to read them.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can read the chord figures and say which note is at the bottom.

  2. 5 min

    What they already know: the chord and the degrees

    Play Z C B and X V N yourself. “Major or minor?” Write on the board the degrees of C major with their chords: I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi.

  3. 17 min

    New material: inversions, the small number and V7

    1. Inversions (5 min). Play the chord of C yourself in its three positions: Z C B, C B A, B A D. The class sings the bottom note of each one: C, E, G.
    2. The small number (6 min). Write I, I6 and I⁶₄ on the board under the three positions. “The small number tells us which note is at the bottom.” Play one position at random; the class says “no number”, “six” or “six-four”.
    3. V7 (6 min). Play B M S (the V) and then add the F: “What changed?” It sounds more tense, it wants to go back. Then play M F G and resolve to A D G: “question and answer”.
  4. 20 min

    Application: the machine’s piano

    Reading the chord figures (6 min). Open the harmony machine with PIANO, CLASSICAL and LEVEL 2 · CHORDS, press SPIN and PLAY. The class reads the figures of each bar and says which note is at the bottom in the left hand.

    The dictation (10 min). Hand out the sheets printed with BLANK. Press SPIN without showing the screen and play the piece two or three times. Under each bar, each pupil writes the Roman numeral they hear: I, IV or V. The quick ones add the small number too.

    Comparing (4 min). Show the screen. Everyone checks their sheet against the machine’s.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Play the four chords of the path yourself; the class says the figures for each one.

    “What does the Roman numeral tell us? And the small number? Why does the piano use inversions?”

    Listen to the answers: the step of the key; which note is at the bottom; so that the hand hardly moves.

Challenge for the quick ones: “In the machine, change LEVEL to 3 · BROKEN CHORDS. Do the chord figures change?” No: the notes are the same, they are just played one after another. And one more: “With MODE on m, which degrees change to lower case?”

With the harmony machine: chord figures on the piano

Open the harmony machine. At the top press PIANO, choose the period CLASSICAL and LEVEL 2 · CHORDS. Press SPIN and then PLAY. You don’t need an account to listen.

  1. The two clefs. Show the screen: it is a piece for piano, two hands. At the top, the right hand plays the melody in the treble clef; at the bottom, the left hand plays one chord per bar in the bass clef, the one they met in Lesson 7. Under each bar are its chord figures: I, IV⁶₄, V⁶₅, the ones they learned today.
  2. Day or night? Press PLAY: in each bar the class says whether the chord is major or minor. Change MODE to m and play again: the same piece, in minor.
  3. Three ways to accompany. LEVEL changes what the left hand does, and the class listens to the difference. 1 · NOTES: a single low note per bar, like the Seikilos drone. 2 · CHORDS: the whole chord, as they played it today. 3 · BROKEN CHORDS: the chord broken up. With LEFT HAND you choose ALBERTI, the accompaniment of Haydn and Mozart: bottom, top, middle, top (C–G–E–G), in quavers; or WALTZ, the bass on the first beat and the chord on the others, like a waltz.

The periods. At the top you choose the period, and the same idea changes its clothes: BAROQUE (Bach), CLASSICAL (Haydn and Mozart), ROMANTIC, IMPRESSIONIST (Debussy) and JAZZ. For this lesson CLASSICAL is enough. With FINGERING you can see the finger numbers, in case someone in the class plays the piano.

Two sets of symbols, two questions. If you choose JAZZ, a second set of chord symbols appears, and it is the first time they see it. Underneath are still the Roman numerals: they tell you which step of the key the chord is on (ii, V, I). Above are letters such as Dm7, G7 or Cmaj7: they tell you what the chord is called, its root note (D, G, C) and its colour, the way jazz and pop musicians read them. In C major, Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 is ii – V – I. If the piece changes key, the Roman numerals do not change; the letters do. A question for the class: “the one underneath tells us where the chord lives; the one above, what it is called”.

To print the piece: press OPEN IN SCORE; in the Score that opens, press PAGE and then PRINT. The sheet comes out ready, with nothing to adjust: the staves, the piano brace and the Roman numerals under each chord. The harmony machine does not print by itself.

Manuscript paper: the BLANK button, next to SPIN, opens the same sheet but empty, with the key and number of bars that are selected. Print it so that each pupil can write under each bar the Roman numeral they hear, or their own chord in the bass clef.

In the next lesson the groups throw Mozart’s dice and give each bar its chord, as in this machine.

In the workbook: chapter 11

The pupil’s workbook, My Little Music Grammar · Level 2, covers this lesson in two pages. It opens with an illustration of two ways to accompany: a Scottish piper, whose drone always sounds the same note (like the kithara drone in Lesson 7), and two children at the piano, whose left hand changes chord. Then come inversions, the small number (I, I6, I⁶₄) and V7, as in this guide.

At the end, the workbook introduces chord symbols with letters from pop music and jazz: C, Dm, Em, F, G7, Am and B°, one for each degree of C major, with an illustration of a saxophone and a piano at a school party, where the symbols C · Am · Dm · G7 rise up from the piano. It is the same idea of letters above Roman numerals that this guide shows with the harmony machine: the Roman numeral says where the chord lives; the letter, what it is called.

If they find it hard:
  • They can’t hear which note is at the bottom: have them sing only the lowest one; you play it on its own before the chord.
  • They confuse 6 and ⁶₄: one number, the third at the bottom; two numbers, the fifth at the bottom.
  • The dictation goes too fast: lower the machine’s tempo and play only the left hand first.
How to tell it worked: most of the class says which note is at the bottom in the four chords of the path; in the dictation, at least three out of four pupils get I and V right; and most can explain what inversions are for on the piano.

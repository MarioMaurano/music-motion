In the classroom — Don Quixote sets off

Don Quixote sets off

Level 2 · Lesson 3b · review of chapters 1 to 3 · 3/8 time, Rocinante’s gallop and the donkey’s walk · Telemann’s suite Burlesque de Quixotte

Teacher’s guide. © 2026 Music Motion.

For: ages 9 to 14, the whole class and then in pairs. Length: 50 minutes.

Main objective: by the end of the lesson, each pair writes a bar of 3/8 whose note values add up to three quavers, claps it and plays it on the Keyboard; the class says whether it sounds like Rocinante’s gallop or the donkey’s walk.

Secondary objectives: (1) recognise by ear, in Telemann’s suite, Rocinante’s gallop and Sancho’s donkey, and move to each one; (2) say how many semiquavers fit in a bar of 3/8 (6) and how many the dotted quaver is worth (3).

What you need: a computer with speakers and internet, Telemann’s suite in the Score, the Cosmic Keyboard, room to move and, if possible, a projector. It helps to have done Lessons 2 and 3 (the dot, semiquavers and fast notes).

Pupil’s workbook: My Little Music Grammar · Level 2: the review of chapters 1 to 3, “Don Quixote sets off”, pages 17 and 18. If the pupils don’t have the booklet, you can print those pages from the workbook PDF.

If a button is not clear: the user guide explains each one with pictures. At the top it has a search box: type, for example, “pulse”, “print” or “wav”.

Before the lesson (10 minutes, at home): practise.

  • Open the suite in the Score. Each piece has its title above the staff. Find “El Galope de Rosinante” (bar 263) and “El Burro de Sancho” (bar 295) and listen to them one after the other. Listen also to “El Ataque a los Molinos de Viento”, the attack on the windmills (bar 180).
  • Clap the two rhythms. Rocinante: four semiquavers and a quaver, “ta-ka-ta-ka-tan”. The donkey: a dotted quaver and three semiquavers, one long and three short, “taaa-ka-ta-ka”.
  • On the Keyboard, play both rhythms on a single key, D.

Before we start: a book, some music and three quavers

The book and the music

In 1605, in Spain, Miguel de Cervantes published the story of Don Quixote, a gentleman who read so many books about knights that he set off in search of adventure, in an old suit of armour, riding Rocinante, a terribly skinny horse. His neighbour Sancho Panza goes with him, on his donkey.

More than a hundred years later, in Germany, Georg Philipp Telemann (1681–1767) turned the book into music: the suite for strings Burlesque de Quixotte, in which each piece tells an adventure. In the Score each piece has its title (in Spanish) and its first bar:

  • Overture (bar 1)
  • Don Quixote wakes up (bar 131)
  • The attack on the windmills (bar 180)
  • Sighs of love for Princess Dulcinea (bar 210)
  • Sancho Panza’s disappointment (bar 240)
  • Rocinante’s gallop (bar 263)
  • Sancho’s donkey (bar 295)

3/8 time and the two gallops

In the gallop and in the donkey, every bar has three quavers: 3/8 time. Since each quaver is worth two semiquavers, six semiquavers fit in one bar.

  • Rocinante gallops with four semiquavers and a quaver: 4 + 2 = 6. Light, as if jumping: “ta-ka-ta-ka-tan”.
  • Sancho’s donkey plods along with a dotted quaver and three semiquavers. The dot adds half (Lesson 2): the dotted quaver is worth three semiquavers, and 3 + 3 = 6. At its own pace: one long and three short.

Both bars are worth the same; what changes is the order of long and short. That is why one jumps and the other walks.

The lesson, step by step

The lesson follows five stages: the goal, what they already know, the new material, the application and the review of the goal. In each activity, you do it first and the class follows you.

  1. 3 min

    The goal

    Tell the class, in these words or similar ones:

    “More than four hundred years ago, a gentleman set off in search of adventure on a terribly skinny horse. Today we are going to hear how it gallops and write our own gallop.”

    Write the goal on the board: I can write and play a galloping bar of three quavers.

  2. 7 min

    What they already know: the chocolate bar and the dot

    On the board: a quaver, a semiquaver, a dotted quaver. “How many semiquavers is each one worth?” Two, one, three. The class claps “ti-ti” and “ti-ki-ti-ki”. In one sentence, recall the three chapters this journey reviews: the path of a piece, one piece and a half, fast notes.

  3. 15 min

    New material: the book, the music and the two gallops

    1. The story (4 min). Show the picture on page 17 and tell, or read, who Don Quixote, Rocinante and Sancho are, and how Telemann turned the book into music.
    2. Listening (5 min). Open the suite in the Score at “El Galope de Rosinante” (bar 263) and let it play on to the donkey (bar 295). The class raises a hand when the music changes: “Who has arrived?”
    3. The two rhythms (6 min). Write the two bars on the board, as on page 17. Clap them first; the class repeats. Count the semiquavers of each together: 4 + 2 = 6 and 3 + 3 = 6. Play them on the D key of the Keyboard.
  4. 20 min

    Application: the dance and our gallop

    The dance (8 min). Divide the class into knights and squires. To Rocinante’s gallop, the knights jump lightly; to Sancho’s donkey, the squires walk at their own pace, one long and three short. Play the music from bar 263 and, when it changes, the steps change.

    Our gallop (12 min). In pairs, they answer questions 1 and 2 on page 18 and then write their own bar of 3/8 (question 3), with note values that add up to three quavers. Write one on the board first as an example. Each pair claps it and plays it on the D key; the class says whether it sounds like Rocinante, the donkey or something else.

  5. 5 min

    Reviewing the goal

    Go back to the sentence on the board. Two or three pairs play their bar.

    “How many quavers are there in a bar of 3/8? How many semiquavers is the dotted quaver worth? Why does Rocinante jump and the donkey walk, if both bars are worth the same?”

    Listen for the answers: three; three; because the order of long and short changes.

A challenge for the quick ones: “Listen to the attack on the windmills (bar 180). Can you hear the sails turning? Which note values does Telemann use to make them turn?” And question 4 on page 18: “Telemann wrote music for the windmills. What other adventure would you like to hear?”

In the workbook: the review of chapters 1 to 3

Pages 17 and 18 open with a picture of Don Quixote on Rocinante and Sancho Panza on his donkey, crossing the fields of La Mancha, and the story of the book and the suite. Below are the two bars: above, Rocinante’s gallop; below, Sancho’s donkey.

In Let’s play!: (1) Rocinante’s first bar has four semiquavers, worth two quavers together; (2) the donkey’s dotted quaver is worth three semiquavers; (3) each pupil writes a galloping bar; (4) the adventure challenge. Then come Let’s dance!, this lesson’s dance; Canelo says, who also gallops “ta-ka-ta-ka-tan” after his ball; Did you know…?, on literature and music; and Let’s listen, which points to Telemann’s suite.

If they find it hard:
  • They think the dot is another note: the dot does not sound on its own; it makes the quaver longer. Hold the key down while counting “one, two, three” in semiquavers.
  • The gallop speeds up: lower the tempo in the Score and clap on the quaver pulse.
  • They don’t hear the piece change: stop the music at bar 295 and start again there; compare the two beginnings.
  • Their bar doesn’t add up to three quavers: have them count in semiquavers; they must reach six.
How to tell it worked: at least three out of four pairs write a bar of 3/8 that adds up to three quavers and play it on the Keyboard; the class recognises the gallop and the donkey by ear and changes step when the music changes; most say that the dotted quaver is worth three semiquavers.
